# Analysis of the 1989 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (March 2, 1990)

## The Performance: 44.4% and a Warning About Double-Dipping

Net worth gain: **$1.515 billion**, or **44.4%**. Over 25 years: $19.46 → **$4,296.01** per share, **23.8% compounded annually**.

But Buffett immediately reframes. Intrinsic value — "the figure indicating what all of our constituent businesses are rationally worth" — is the real measure, and he defines it precisely:

> "With perfect foresight, this number can be calculated by taking all future cash flows of a business — in and out — and discounting them at prevailing interest rates. So valued, all businesses, from manufacturers of buggy whips to operators of cellular phones, become economic equals."

Then the crucial admission about *why* the past won't repeat:

> "Our performance to date has benefited from a double-dip: (1) the exceptional gains in intrinsic value that our portfolio companies have achieved; (2) the additional bonus we realized as the market appropriately 'corrected' the prices of these companies... But our 'catch-up' rewards have been realized, which means we'll have to settle for a single-dip in the future."

This is important: he's saying the *valuation* tailwind is exhausted. The businesses can keep compounding; the multiple expansion cannot keep helping.

## The Sagan Bacteria: Growth Forges Its Own Anchor

The Carl Sagan riff is one of his best:

> "Although a bacterium weighs only about a trillionth of a gram, its descendants, after a day of wild asexual abandon, will collectively weigh as much as a mountain...in two days, more than the sun — and before very long, everything in the universe will be made of bacteria."

Of course, "the bugs run out of food, or they poison each other, or they are shy about reproducing in public."

> "Even on bad days, Charlie Munger and I do not think of Berkshire as a bacterium. Nor, to our unending sorrow, have we found a way to double its net worth every 15 minutes. Furthermore, we are not the least bit shy about reproducing — financially — in public."

## The Tax Essay: The 20-Year Thought Experiment

This is one of the most underrated passages in the entire corpus. Buffett explains that the deferred tax liability is "an interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury that comes due only at our election."

Then the arithmetic:

- **Scenario 1:** $1 doubles, is sold, after-tax proceeds reinvested — repeat for 20 years at 34% capital gains tax. Result: **$25,250** to you, **$13,000** to the government.
- **Scenario 2:** $1 doubles 20 times *without selling*. Result: **$1,048,576**, of which **$692,000** is yours after tax.

> "The sole reason for this staggering difference in results would be the timing of tax payments. Interestingly, the government would gain from Scenario 2 in exactly the same 27:1 ratio as we — taking in taxes of $356,500 vs. $13,000 — though, admittedly, it would have to wait for its money."

He stresses they didn't adopt the long-holding strategy *because* of this math — "it is possible we could earn greater after-tax returns by moving rather frequently." The real reason is temperamental and human:

> "We have found splendid business relationships to be so rare and so enjoyable that we want to retain all we develop... That would be akin to marrying for money — a mistake under most circumstances, insanity if one is already rich."

## Look-Through Earnings: $45M Dividends vs. $212M Retained

The five major investees paid Berkshire about **$45 million** in dividends in 1989. Berkshire's share of their **retained** earnings was about **$212 million** — not counting large capital gains at GEICO and Coca-Cola.

> "If this $212 million had been distributed to us, our own operating earnings, after the payment of additional taxes, would have been close to $500 million rather than the $300 million shown in the table."

And the key judgment:

> "The question you must decide is whether these undistributed earnings are as valuable to us as those we report. We believe they are — and even think they may be more valuable... earnings retained by these investees will be deployed by talented, owner-oriented managers who sometimes have better uses for these funds in their own businesses than we would have in ours."

Note the caveat: "I would not make such a generous assessment of most managements, but it is appropriate in these cases."

## The Sainted Seven Plus One: 57% on Equity

Borsheim's joined the group. The eight businesses earned **57% on average equity capital** with **no net leverage**.

> "When I call off the names of our managers — the Blumkin, Friedman and Heldman families, Chuck Huggins, Stan Lipsey, and Ralph Schey — I feel the same glow that Miller Huggins must have experienced when he announced the lineup of his 1927 New York Yankees."

**The Ike Friedman story** is the letter's best joke:

> Ike brought $20 million of jewels to a Santa Fe gathering. Buffett worried about security. Ike pointed to a safe: "This afternoon we changed the combination and now even the hotel management doesn't know what it is." Two armed guards would watch it all night. Then: *"And besides, Warren, the jewels aren't in the safe."*

> "How can we miss with a fellow like that?"

## Mrs. B Quits — and Starts Again

The sad note: **Rose Blumkin, 96, quit NFM in May** after disagreeing with the family about the carpet department. Buffett sides with the family — carpet sales had declined 17% over three years while other departments grew 24%.

But:

> "You will be pleased to know that Mrs. B continues to make Horatio Alger's heroes look like victims of tired blood. At age 96 she has started a new business selling — what else? — carpet and furniture. And as always, she works seven days a week."

The Blumkins' carpet share in Omaha: **75.3%**, up from 67.7%, "over six times that of its nearest competitor."

## Insurance: Capacity Is a Mental State

The best structural insight in the letter:

> "At bottom, therefore, the amount of industry capacity at any particular moment primarily depends on the mental state of insurance managers."

And:

> "Good profits will be realized only when there is a shortage of capacity. Shortages will occur only when insurers are frightened. That happens rarely — and most assuredly is not happening now."

He also dismantles the "underwriting cycle" language — it was meaningful only during the cartel era, when rate bureaus and multi-year policies created rhythmic lags. That's gone.

**The CAT cover story** is the operational highlight. After Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake, many insurers were "bare." Berkshire instantly offered up to **$250 million** of catastrophe coverage.

> "Our willingness to put such a huge sum on the line for a loss that could occur tomorrow sets us apart from any reinsurer in the world."

Why can they? Because they retain the risk rather than laying it off:

> "(1) by the standards of regulatory accounting, we have a net worth in our insurance companies of about $6 billion...; and (2) we simply don't care what earnings we report quarterly, or even annually, just as long as the decisions leading to those earnings (or losses) were reached intelligently."

And the managerial insight:

> "What is best for their owners is not necessarily best for the managers... We are willing to look foolish as long as we don't feel we have acted foolishly."

## Coca-Cola: The 52-Year Delay

Holdings grew from 14.17M to **23.35M shares**. And then the self-deprecating masterpiece:

> "I believe I had my first Coca-Cola in either 1935 or 1936... I continued to note these qualities for the next 52 years as Coke blanketed the world. During this period, however, I carefully avoided buying even a single share, instead allocating major portions of my net worth to street railway companies, windmill manufacturers, anthracite producers, textile businesses, trading-stamp issuers, and the like. (If you think I'm making this up, I can supply the names.) Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes."

> "My response time to the next glaringly attractive idea will be slashed to well under 50 years."

He credits Roberto Goizueta and Don Keough: "a truly rare blend of marketing and financial skills... the mesh of marketing and finance is perfect and the result is a shareholder's dream."

## The Convertible Preferreds

Four positions: **Salomon** ($700M), **Gillette** ($600M, 8¾%, convertible at $50), **USAir** ($358M, 9¼%, convertible at $60), **Champion** ($300M, 9¼%, convertible at $38).

The honesty here matters. He admits he can't forecast investment banking, airlines, or paper:

> "This does not mean that we predict a negative future for these industries: we're agnostics, not atheists. Our lack of strong convictions about these businesses, however, means that we must structure our investments in them differently."

The structure gives downside protection plus upside participation — but he's clear about the cost:

> "Under almost any conditions, we expect these preferreds to return us our money plus dividends. If that is all we get, though, the result will be disappointing, because we will have given up flexibility."

He also notes the investees insisted Berkshire's preferreds have **unrestricted voting rights on a fully-converted basis** — "far from standard in corporate finance." They were trusting Berkshire to be an intelligent long-term owner, and Berkshire was trusting them to be intelligent long-term managers.

## The Zero-Coupon Essay: The Letter's Masterpiece

This is the longest and most important section. It deserves careful attention because it's Buffett at his most forensic.

### The benign history
- **Series E Savings Bonds** (WWII) — zero-coupon in substance, 2.9% compounded, no market risk, one in two households held them
- **STRIPS** — Treasury coupons stripped and sold as individual zero-coupon bonds, solving the reinvestment risk problem for pension funds and IRAs

> "But as happens in Wall Street all too often, what the wise do in the beginning, fools do in the end."

### The corruption
Zero-coupon and PIK bonds issued in enormous quantities by "ever-junkier credits."

> "To these issuers, zero (or PIK) bonds offer one overwhelming advantage: It is impossible to default on a promise to pay nothing. Indeed, if LDC governments had issued no debt in the 1970's other than long-term zero-coupon obligations, they would now have a spotless record as debtors."

### The ratchet of standards
1. First: conservatively estimated free cash flow had to cover interest **and** modest debt reduction
2. Then: prices rose so high all free cash flow went to interest, leaving nothing for principal. "Debt now became something to be refinanced rather than repaid."
3. Then: **EBDIT** — Earnings Before Depreciation, Interest and Taxes — as the test

> "Such an attitude is clearly delusional. At 95% of American businesses, capital expenditures that over time roughly approximate depreciation are a necessity and are every bit as real an expense as labor or utility costs. Even a high school dropout knows that to finance a car he must have income that covers not only interest and operating expenses, but also realistically-calculated depreciation. He would be laughed out of the bank if he started talking about EBDIT."

4. Then: EBDIT measured against **cash interest only**, ignoring accrued interest on zeros and PIKs

> "To their shame, many professional investment managers went along with this nonsense, though they usually were careful to do so only with clients' money, not their own. (Calling these managers 'professionals' is actually too kind; they should be designated 'promotees.')"

### The bezzle
Galbraith's term — "the current amount of undiscovered embezzlement."

> "The embezzlers are richer by the amount of the bezzle, while the embezzlees do not yet feel poorer."

> "With zeros, one party to a contract can experience 'income' without his opposite experiencing the pain of expenditure... As long as major investors willingly don their Peter Pan wings and repeatedly say 'I believe,' there is no limit to how much 'income' can be created by the zero-coupon bond."

### The alchemy
> "In the end, alchemy, whether it is metallurgical or financial, fails. A base business can not be transformed into a golden business by tricks of accounting or capital structure. The man claiming to be a financial alchemist may become rich. But gullible investors rather than business achievements will usually be the source of his wealth."

### The bartender standard
> "Promoters, after all, have throughout time exercised the same judgment and restraint in accepting money that alcoholics have exercised in accepting liquor. At a minimum, therefore, the banker's conduct should rise to that of a responsible bartender who, when necessary, refuses the profit from the next drink to avoid sending a drunk out on the highway. In recent years, unfortunately, many leading investment firms have found bartender morality to be an intolerably restrictive standard."

### The taxpayer footnote
S&Ls bought zeros with FSLIC-insured deposits, "recorded — but did not receive — ultra-high interest income."

> "Had their loans to shaky credits worked, the owners of the associations would have pocketed the profits. In the many cases in which the loans will fail, the taxpayer will pick up the bill. To paraphrase Jackie Mason, at these associations it was the managers who should have been wearing the ski masks."

**The practical test he offers:**

> "Whenever an investment banker starts talking about EBDIT — or whenever someone creates a capital structure that does not allow all interest, both payable and accrued, to be comfortably met out of current cash flow net of ample capital expenditures — zip up your wallet."

## The Mistakes of the First Twenty-Five Years

This is the section the letter is famous for. Seven admissions:

### 1. Buying Berkshire itself
> "I was enticed to buy because the price looked cheap."

The **cigar butt** metaphor:

> "A cigar butt found on the street that has only one puff left in it may not offer much of a smoke, but the 'bargain purchase' will make that puff all profit."

Why it fails:

> "First, the original 'bargain' price probably will not turn out to be such a steal after all. In a difficult business, no sooner is one problem solved than another surfaces — never is there just one cockroach in the kitchen. Second, any initial advantage you secure will be quickly eroded by the low return that the business earns."

> "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre."

The **Hochschild Kohn** example — bought at a discount to book, first-class people, unrecorded real estate, LIFO cushion. Sold three years later for about what he paid.

> "I had memories like those of the husband in the country song, 'My Wife Ran Away With My Best Friend and I Still Miss Him a Lot.'"

> "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner."

### 2. Good jockeys on broken-down nags
Berkshire's textile business and Hochschild Kohn both had able, honest managers. They were "running in quicksand."

> "I just wish I hadn't been so energetic in creating examples. My behavior has matched that admitted by Mae West: 'I was Snow White, but I drifted.'"

### 3. Easy does it
> "After 25 years of buying and supervising a great variety of businesses, Charlie and I have not learned how to solve difficult business problems. What we have learned is to avoid them."

> "We concentrated on identifying one-foot hurdles that we could step over rather than because we acquired any ability to clear seven-footers."

> "Overall, however, we've done better by avoiding dragons than by slaying them."

### 4. The institutional imperative
The most surprising discovery. Four symptoms:

> "(1) As if governed by Newton's First Law of Motion, an institution will resist any change in its current direction; (2) Just as work expands to fill available time, corporate projects or acquisitions will materialize to soak up available funds; (3) Any business craving of the leader, however foolish, will be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and strategic studies prepared by his troops; and (4) The behavior of peer companies, whether they are expanding, acquiring, setting executive compensation or whatever, will be mindlessly imitated."

> "Institutional dynamics, not venality or stupidity, set businesses on these courses."

### 5. Only with people you like, trust, and admire
> "A second-class textile or department-store company won't prosper simply because its managers are men that you would be pleased to see your daughter marry."

> "We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad person."

### 6. Mistakes of omission
> "Some of my worst mistakes were not publicly visible. These were stock and business purchases whose virtues I understood and yet didn't make... I have passed on a couple of really big purchases that were served up to me on a platter and that I was fully capable of understanding. For Berkshire's shareholders, myself included, the cost of this thumb-sucking has been huge."

### 7. Conservative leverage — not a mistake
The 99:1 framing:

> "We wouldn't have liked those 99:1 odds — and never will. A small chance of distress or disgrace cannot, in our view, be offset by a large chance of extra returns."

> "Charlie and I have never been in a big hurry: We enjoy the process far more than the proceeds — though we have learned to live with those also."

And the closer: "We hope in another 25 years to report on the mistakes of the first 50."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1989 Edition)

**1. He defines intrinsic value operationally, not poetically.** Discounted future cash flows — and "all businesses... become economic equals."

**2. He distinguishes between the business compounding and the multiple expanding.** The "double-dip" is over. This is a rare, explicit acknowledgment that past returns contained a non-repeatable component.

**3. He thinks in after-tax, compounding terms — and shows the math.** The 20-year doubling example is the clearest illustration he ever gave of why holding periods matter.

**4. He uses structure when he lacks conviction.** The convertible preferreds exist because he can't forecast airlines or paper. He buys a contract, not a business, when his knowledge is insufficient.

**5. He is a forensic critic of financial engineering.** The zero-coupon essay is a complete anatomy of how accounting can manufacture income, and it names the incentive structure that produced it.

**6. He treats mistakes as the curriculum.** The 25-year section is not performative humility — it's a taxonomy: cheap-price folly, jockey-on-nag, hard problems, institutional imperative, bad partners, omission, leverage. Each has a lesson attached.

**7. He values the process over the proceeds.** Repeated three times in different forms: the tax essay, the leverage section, and the "marrying for money" line.

## The Key Quote

> "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price."

Everyone quotes this. What's new in the 1989 letter is the *evidence* behind it — the cigar butt, Hochschild Kohn, the textile mills, the cockroaches — and the acknowledgment that it took him decades to learn.

But the most important passage may be this one:

> "After 25 years of buying and supervising a great variety of businesses, Charlie and I have not learned how to solve difficult business problems. What we have learned is to avoid them."

That's not modesty. It's strategy. The whole architecture of Berkshire — the acquisition criteria, the "we don't supply management," the aversion to turnarounds, the preference for simple businesses — is downstream of that single sentence.
