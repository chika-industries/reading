# Analysis of the 1984 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 25, 1985)

## The Performance: Mediocre by Berkshire Standards

Net worth gain: **$152.6 million** or **$133 per share**. But Buffett calls this "mediocre" – because the gain was only **13.6%** on beginning net worth, far below the 22.1% twenty-year compounded rate (from $19.46 in 1964 to $1,108.77 in 1984).

> "Economic gains must be evaluated by comparison with the capital that produces them."

The drag of a mushrooming capital base is no longer academic:

> "Using my academic voice, I have told you in the past of the drag that a mushrooming capital base exerts upon rates of return. Unfortunately, my academic voice is now giving way to a reportorial voice."

To earn even 15% annually over the next decade would require profits of about **$3.9 billion**.

> "Accomplishing this will require a few big ideas – small ones just won't do. Charlie Munger, my partner in general management, and I do not have any such ideas at present, but our experience has been that they pop up occasionally. (How's that for a strategic plan?)"

## The GEICO and General Foods Redemption Disputes

A fascinating accounting battle. In 1983, GEICO made a tender offer; Berkshire sold shares proportionately, keeping ownership exactly the same. The $21 million received was treated as a dividend (taxed at 6.9% inter-corporate rate rather than 28% capital gains).

In 1984, virtually the same transaction with General Foods. But Peat Marwick's New York office disagreed with Omaha and Chicago, insisting the transactions be treated as sales of stock rather than dividends. To avoid a qualified opinion, Buffett adopted the New York view – though he disagrees with it on both economic substance and proper accounting.

> "None of this, however, has any effect on intrinsic business value."

## The Case for Share Repurchases (and Against Greenmail)

Buffett makes one of his strongest arguments for repurchases:

> "When companies with outstanding businesses and comfortable financial positions find their shares selling far below intrinsic value in the marketplace, no alternative action can benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases."

The obvious benefit: "major repurchases at prices well below per-share intrinsic business value immediately increase, in a highly significant way, that value. When companies purchase their own stock, they often find it easy to get $2 of present value for $1."

The subtle benefit: management demonstrates it is pro-shareholder rather than pro-empire. "Shareholders and potential shareholders increase their estimates of future returns from the business. This upward revision, in turn, produces market prices more in line with intrinsic business value."

> "The key word is 'demonstrated'. A manager who consistently turns his back on repurchases, when these clearly are in the interests of owners, reveals more than he knows of his motivations. No matter how often or how eloquently he mouths some public relations-inspired phrase such as 'maximizing shareholder wealth'... the market correctly discounts assets lodged with him. His heart is not listening to his mouth – and, after a while, neither will the market."

**Greenmail is "odious and repugnant":** The three players are (1) the "shareholder" extortionist, (2) corporate insiders who seek peace at any price – as long as the price is paid by someone else, and (3) the innocent shareholders whose money funds the payoff. "As the dust settles, the mugging, transient shareholder gives his speech on 'free enterprise', the muggee management gives its speech on 'the best interests of the company', and the innocent shareholder standing by mutely funds the payoff."

## Nebraska Furniture Mart: The Blumkin Family

Buffett understated the case last year: "I can honestly say that I never have seen a managerial group that either functions or behaves better than the Blumkin family."

Mrs. B, now 91: "I come home to eat and sleep, and that's about it. I can't wait until it gets daylight so I can get back to the business."

She received an Honorary Doctorate in Commercial Science from NYU – without ever having spent a day in a school room. Previous recipients include the CEOs of Exxon, Citicorp, IBM, GM, and Paul Volcker. "They are in good company."

**The economics:** NFM's gross margin is not much more than half of Levitz's 44.4%. Operating expenses are 16.5% of sales versus 35.6% at Levitz.

> "By unparalleled efficiency and astute volume purchasing, NFM is able to earn excellent returns on capital while saving its customers at least $30 million annually."

**The secrets:** (1) enthusiasm and energy that would make Ben Franklin and Horatio Alger look like dropouts; (2) define with extraordinary realism their area of special competence; (3) ignore even the most enticing propositions outside that area; (4) unfailingly behave in a high-grade manner with everyone. "Mrs. B boils it down to 'sell cheap and tell the truth'."

**The trust:** When Berkshire purchased 90% of NFM, there was no audit, no inventory, no verification of receivables, no property title check.

> "We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even exchange."

## See's Candy: Exceptional Product, Exceptional Manager

| Year | Sales | After-Tax Profits | Pounds Sold | Stores |
|------|-------|-------------------|-------------|--------|
| 1984 | $135.9M | $13.4M | 24.8M | 214 |
| 1972 | $31.3M | $2.1M | 17.0M | 167 |

> "This performance has not been produced by a generally rising tide. To the contrary, many well-known participants in the boxed-chocolate industry either have lost money in this same period or have been marginally profitable."

Same-store volume declined 1.1%. Total shop volume grew 0.6% due to new stores. The business gets more seasonal: 40% of volume and 75% of profits in the four weeks before Christmas.

**Product integrity:** Competitors add preservatives or freeze product to smooth production cycles. "We reject such techniques, opting, in effect, for production headaches rather than product modification."

## Buffalo Evening News: Dominant Newspaper Economics

Profits exceeded expectations. Costs increased only 4.9% due to productivity improvements. But new union contracts with "catch-up" wage increases will raise costs in 1985. Newsprint prices also less favorable.

**Two major economic advantages:**
1. Circulation concentrated in area of maximum utility to advertisers (unlike "regional" newspapers with wasted circulation)
2. Exceptional penetration of Buffalo retail market

**Penetration:** Number one in weekday penetration among the 100 largest papers; number two on Sunday.

**News hole:** 50.9% in 1984 (vs. typical 35-40%). "We will continue to maintain this ratio in the 50% area."

> "The economics of a dominant newspaper are excellent, among the very best in the business world. Owners, naturally, would like to believe that their wonderful profitability is achieved only because they unfailingly turn out a wonderful product. That comfortable theory wilts before an uncomfortable fact. While first-class newspapers make excellent profits, the profits of third-rate papers are as good or better – as long as either class of paper is dominant within its community."

> "Once dominant, the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how good or how bad the paper will be. Good or bad, it will prosper."

## Insurance: A Humbling 134 Combined Ratio

Industry combined ratio: 117.7. Berkshire's: **134**. "This is the third year in a row that our underwriting performance has been far poorer than that of the industry."

Buffett expects improvement in 1985, "substantially greater than that of the industry." Mike Goldberg has corrected many mistakes. Competition shaken, prices raised significantly in certain lines.

> "For some years I have told you that there could be a day coming when our premier financial strength would make a real difference in the competitive position of our insurance operation. That day may have arrived."

> "The buyer of insurance receives only a promise in exchange for his cash. The value of that promise should be appraised against the possibility of adversity, not prosperity."

**Structured settlements and loss reserve assumptions:** Growing from $16.2M to $30.6M. "Very few companies in the property/casualty field can meet this test of unquestioned long-term strength."

## GEICO: The Crown Jewel

GEICO's combined ratio remains far better than industry. Berkshire's 36% interest gives it $320 million of GEICO's direct volume – "well over double our own premium volume."

> "In 1984... the carrying value of our interest in GEICO changed hardly at all, while the intrinsic business value of that interest increased substantially. Since 27% of Berkshire's net worth at the beginning of 1984 was represented by GEICO, its static market value had a significant impact upon our rate of gain for the year. We are not at all unhappy with such a result: we would far rather have the business value of GEICO increase by X during the year, while market value decreases, than have the intrinsic value increase by only 1/2 X with market value soaring."

> "In its core business – low-cost auto and homeowners insurance – GEICO has a major, sustainable competitive advantage. That is a rare asset in business generally, and it's almost non-existent in the field of financial services."

## Errors in Loss Reserving: The Rented Suit

This is one of Buffett's most candid and important sections. He explains why property/casualty insurance earnings are "a first rough draft of history."

The loss expense charged in any year includes: (1) losses paid during the year; (2) estimates for reported but unsettled losses; (3) IBNR estimates; and (4) revisions of past estimates.

**The rented suit story:** A man traveling abroad learns his father died. He tells his sister to arrange the funeral and send the bill. He pays several thousand dollars. Then monthly bills of $15 arrive. When he asks his sister what's going on: "Oh, I forgot to tell you. We buried Dad in a rented suit."

> "If you've been in the insurance business in recent years – particularly the reinsurance business – this story hurts."

**The reserving error table:**

| Year | Reported | Corrected One Year Later |
|------|----------|--------------------------|
| 1980 | $6,738,000 | $14,887,000 |
| 1981 | $1,478,000 | ($1,118,000) |
| 1982 | ($21,462,000) | ($25,066,000) |
| 1983 | ($33,192,000) | ($50,974,000) |
| 1984 | ($45,413,000) | ? |

> "As you can see from reviewing the table, my errors in reporting to you have been substantial and recently have always presented a better underwriting picture than was truly the case."

Three reasons for chagrin: (1) you should be able to count on what I say; (2) managers acted with less urgency; (3) we paid taxes on overstated earnings.

**The walking dead:** Insolvent insurers don't run out of cash until long after they've run out of net worth. "These 'walking dead' often redouble their efforts to write business, accepting almost any price or risk, simply to keep the cash flowing in."

> "The corpse is supposed to file the death certificate."

## Washington Public Power Supply System (WPPSS): Bonds as a Business

Berkshire purchased $139 million of WPPSS Projects 1, 2, and 3 bonds. The "business" contractually earns **$22.7 million after tax** – available currently in cash. That's a **16.3% after-tax return on unleveraged capital.**

> "We are unable to buy operating businesses with economics close to these. Only a relatively few businesses earn the 16.3% after tax on unleveraged capital that our WPPSS investment does and those businesses, when available for purchase, sell at large premiums to that capital."

**The risks:** Very slight risk of total loss; risk of interest interruption; ceiling on upside (face value $205 million, only 48% above cost).

**The bond-as-business framework:** Buffett compares buying a 12% zero-coupon bond for $10 million (worth $300 million in 30 years) to a business earning 12% on equity and retaining all earnings (also $300 million in 30 years).

> "We believe that many staggering errors by investors could have been avoided if they had viewed bond investment with a businessman's perspective."

In 1946, 20-year AAA tax-exempts yielded slightly below 1%. "The buyer of those bonds... bought a 'business' that earned about 1% on 'book value'... and paid 100 cents on the dollar for that abominable business."

> "In what I think is by far the best book on investing ever written – 'The Intelligent Investor', by Ben Graham – the last section of the last chapter begins with, 'Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike.'"

**The lemming analogy:** Most managers have no incentive to make intelligent-but-risky decisions. "Failing conventionally is the route to go; as a group, lemmings may have a rotten image, but no individual lemming has ever received bad press."

> "With 47% of Berkshire's stock, Charlie and I don't worry about being fired, and we receive our rewards as owners, not managers. Thus we behave with Berkshire's money as we would with our own."

**Concentration:** Billy Rose: "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well." Concentration makes sense only because of exceptional financial strength. "Over time our policy of concentration should produce superior results, though these will be tempered by our large size."

**Inflation warning:** "We believe substantial inflation lies ahead... Furthermore, we think there is a small, but not insignificant, chance of runaway inflation." Present fiscal policy is "both extremely dangerous and difficult to reverse." Charlie Brown: "No problem is so big that it can't be run away from."

> "Bonds are as sound as a dollar – and we view the long-term outlook for dollars as dismal."

## Dividend Policy: The Most Important Section

This is one of Buffett's most thorough treatments of capital allocation. He distinguishes between **restricted earnings** (must be retained to maintain the business) and **unrestricted earnings** (can be retained or distributed).

**Restricted earnings:** "Inflation causes some or all of the reported earnings to become ersatz." Cannot be distributed without losing ground. "No matter how conservative its payout ratio, a company that consistently distributes restricted earnings is destined for oblivion."

**The Con Edison example:** In the 1970s, Con Ed's stock sold as low as one-fourth of book value – every dollar retained became only 25 cents of market value. Yet most earnings were reinvested. Meanwhile, signs proudly proclaimed: **"Dig We Must."**

**Unrestricted earnings:** Should be retained only when "for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners."

**The 10% perpetual bond analogy:** If interest rates are 5%, you reinvest your 10% coupon. If rates are 15%, you take cash. Owners should wish earnings reinvested if they can earn high returns, paid out if low returns.

**The CEO's schizoid approach:** At the subsidiary level, managers understand capital allocation. At the parent level, they follow historical payout patterns. "Here managers frequently have trouble putting themselves in the shoes of their shareholder-owners."

**The Pro-Am golf analogy:** A company with a wonderful core business can use small amounts of incremental capital at very high returns. Even if all other capital allocation is disastrous, the overall return on retained capital may look excellent. "The managers at fault periodically report on the lessons they have learned from the latest disappointment. They then usually seek out future lessons. (Failure seems to go to their heads.)"

**Berkshire's history:** The three original cornerstone businesses were textiles (survived but earned almost nothing), department stores (shriveled while incurring large losses), and trading stamps (shrunk to 5% of original size).

> "Who says 'you can't lose 'em all'? Only by committing available funds to much better businesses were we able to overcome these origins. (It's been like overcoming a misspent youth.)"

**Current policy:** Retain all earnings as long as prospective returns are above the rate required to produce a dollar of market value per dollar retained. Use retained earnings to build insurance capital. "Large premium-volume gains for the industry are imminent... These circumstances could produce major amounts of profitable business for us."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1984 Edition)

**1. He thinks in terms of capital allocation, not earnings growth.**
The dividend policy section is a masterclass. Every dollar retained must earn its keep. The 10% perpetual bond analogy is brilliant.

**2. He treats bonds as businesses.**
The WPPSS analysis is unusual and powerful. He compares the after-tax return on the bond investment to what he could earn buying an operating business. Bonds are just another way to deploy capital.

**3. He is brutally honest about reserving errors.**
The rented suit story and the one-year-later correction table are painful to read. He takes full blame and explains the systemic problem.

**4. He distinguishes between price and value in repurchases.**
The case for repurchases is simple arithmetic: $2 of present value for $1. The subtle benefit is management signaling.

**5. He despises greenmail.**
The "odious and repugnant" passage is one of his strongest condemnations. The three players – extortionist, insiders, innocent shareholders – are clearly drawn.

**6. He values character above all.**
Mrs. B at 91, still selling carpet. "We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even exchange."

**7. He is preparing for inflation.**
"Substantial inflation lies ahead... a small, but not insignificant, chance of runaway inflation." Bonds are "as sound as a dollar – and we view the long-term outlook for dollars as dismal."

**8. He thinks like an owner.**
"I don't worry about being fired, and we receive our rewards as owners, not managers. Thus we behave with Berkshire's money as we would with our own."

## The Key Quote

> "Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect – backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future – that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners."

This is the shareholder's bill of rights. Every CEO should be required to explain why retained earnings will pass this test. Most cannot.

But the quote that best captures the letter's spirit:

> "With 47% of Berkshire's stock, Charlie and I don't worry about being fired, and we receive our rewards as owners, not managers. Thus we behave with Berkshire's money as we would with our own."

That's the entire secret of Berkshire's success – alignment of interests, owner mentality, long-term thinking. Everything else follows.

## Transition Note

1984 was a year of transition. The insurance underwriting was terrible – Buffett's fault, and he said so. The reserving errors were embarrassing. But the equity portfolio was a rocket. Nebraska Furniture Mart was a joy. The Buffalo News was printing money. And the dividend policy section laid out the definitive framework for capital allocation.

The subsequent event – the Capital Cities/ABC investment – would become one of Buffett's most famous and successful deals. Tom Murphy would become his ideal manager. The seeds of the next decade were planted.

But the most important legacy of the 1984 letter is the **dividend policy section**. It is Buffett's most complete statement of how capital should be allocated – not just at Berkshire, but at any corporation. Every investor should read it. Every manager should be judged by it.
