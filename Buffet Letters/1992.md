# Analysis of the 1992 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (March 1, 1993)

## The Performance: 20.3%, and a Frank Word About the Future

Per-share book value rose **20.3%**. Over 28 years: $19 → **$7,745**, or **23.6% compounded annually**.

Net worth increased $1.52 billion, with **more than 98% coming from earnings and portfolio appreciation**, the rest from share issuance on the convertible debenture call.

**The share count detail is striking:** Berkshire ended 1992 with **1,152,547 shares** outstanding — versus **1,137,778** on October 1, 1964. Twenty-eight years of compounding and the share count is up **1.3%**. That is the cleanest possible statement of the "we only issue shares when we get as much as we give" policy.

## The Bank Anecdote: Growth vs. Owner Wealth

Buffett's illustration of why size and shareholder wealth don't automatically go together is one of his best short stories:

A bank he owned had management "hell-bent on expansion. (Aren't they all?)" It wooed a smaller bank whose owner demanded a swap valuing the acquiree at **over twice** the acquirer's net worth and earning power. Management, "visibly in heat," capitulated. The seller then added one condition:

> "You must promise me that once our merger is done and I have become a major shareholder, you'll never again make a deal this dumb."

## The Volatility Section: Three Points and Two Predictions

**Point 1:** The operating businesses' earnings are unaffected by stock market valuations — and their contribution has shifted from drag (textiles) to engine (the modern collection).

**Point 2 — the structural disadvantage:** Berkshire's securities income and gains bear a corporate tax liability; the S&P returns in the table are pre-tax.

> "Under present tax laws, a gain for the S&P of 18% delivers a corporate holder of that index a return well short of 13%... This is a structural disadvantage we simply have to live with; there is no antidote for it."

**Point 3 — two predictions:**
- The S&P's next decade will be **far worse** than the last
- Berkshire's expanding capital base will **substantially reduce** its historical advantage

> "We've long felt that the only value of stock forecasters is to make fortune tellers look good. Even now, Charlie and I continue to believe that short-term market forecasts are poison and should be kept locked up in a safe place, away from children and also from grown-ups who behave in the market like children."

But the long-term conclusion isn't a forecast in the same sense:

> "It is clear that stocks cannot forever overperform their underlying businesses, as they have so dramatically done for some time."

**The anchor metaphor:** "The only open question is whether we can drag the anchor along at some tolerable, though slowed, pace."

**And the measurement philosophy:**
> "We not only accept this volatility but welcome it: A tolerance for short-term swings improves our long-term prospects. In baseball lingo, our performance yardstick is slugging percentage, not batting average."

That line is doing real work. Batting average rewards consistency; slugging percentage rewards total bases per at-bat, which means it *rewards* swinging for extra bases even at the cost of more strikeouts. That's the super-cat business, the concentrated equity portfolio, and the whole lumpy-15%-over-smooth-12% preference, all in six words.

## Salomon: Stepping Down

Buffett resigned as Interim Chairman in June 1992 after ten months.

> "You can tell from Berkshire's 1991-92 results that the company didn't miss me while I was gone. But the reverse isn't true: I missed Berkshire and am delighted to be back full-time. There is no job in the world that is more fun than running Berkshire."

**The outcome:** Salomon ranked **second of 311 companies** in Fortune's Most Admired survey for improvement in reputation. Salomon Brothers reported **record pre-tax earnings, 34% above the previous high**.

**The credit list:** Deryck Maughan, Bob Denham, Don Howard, John Macfarlane — "without [their] combined efforts... the firm very probably would not have survived." And Ron Olson of Munger, Tolles & Olson, who navigated five authorities (SEC, NY Fed, Treasury, SDNY US Attorney, DOJ Antitrust).

> "If we were to resolve our problems in a coordinated and prompt manner, we needed a lawyer with exceptional legal, business and human skills. Ron had them all."

## Acquisitions: Central States Indemnity and the Toad Doctrine Revisited

> "In the search, we adopt the same attitude one might find appropriate in looking for a spouse: It pays to be active, interested and open-minded, but it does not pay to be in a hurry."

**The toad passage, updated with a new admission:**

> "In my early days as a manager I, too, dated a few toads. They were cheap dates — I've never been much of a sport — but my results matched those of acquirers who courted higher-priced toads. I kissed and they croaked."

**The golf pro's advice:**
> "Practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent."

> "And thereafter I revised my strategy and tried to buy good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices."

**Central States Indemnity:** 82% purchased in December. Credit-card disability/unemployment payment insurer. ~$90M premiums, ~$10M profits. Omaha-based, run by **Bill Kizer**, a friend of 35 years. Family retains 18%.

**The parallel to National Indemnity (1967):** Also Omaha, also an insurer, also bought from a longtime friend, Jack Ringwalt.

> "Jack's comment at the time: 'If I don't sell the company, my executor will, and I'd rather pick the home for it.'"

> "Hollywood has had good luck with sequels; I believe we, too, will."

**The running joke about family size:** H.H. Brown's Frank Rooney has **eight children**; Bill Kizer has **nine**. "It won't be easy to keep this string going in 1993."

**Add-on acquisitions:** Five in 1992, including H.H. Brown's purchase of **Lowell Shoe** ($90M sales, maker of Nursemates).
> "In this manner, we enlarge the domain of managers we already know to be outstanding — and that's a low-risk and high-return proposition."

## Look-Through Earnings: $604 Million, and a $1.8 Billion Target

Look-through earnings rose from **$516M to $604M**. To hit 15% intrinsic value growth, they need to reach **over $1.8 billion by 2000**.

> "We cannot promise to achieve the $1.8 billion target. Indeed, we may not even come close to it. But it does guide our decision-making: When we allocate capital today, we are thinking about what will maximize look-through earnings in 2000."



**The accountability clause** — this is important, because it's where he pre-empts the "long-term" excuse:

> "Just as you should be suspicious of managers who pump up short-term earnings by accounting maneuvers, asset sales and the like, so also should you be suspicious of those managers who fail to deliver for extended periods and blame it on their long-term focus."

> "(Even Alice, after listening to the Queen lecture her about 'jam tomorrow,' finally insisted, 'It must come sometimes to jam today.')"

**The table shows the shift:** Cap Cities $70M, Coke $82M, Freddie Mac $29M (ownership jumped from 3.4% to 8.2%), GEICO $34M (down from $69M, excluding realized gains), **General Dynamics $11M (new)**, Gillette $38M, Guinness $7M, Washington Post $11M, **Wells Fargo $16M (from –$17M)**.

## Insurance: Hurricane Andrew and "Swimming Naked"

Industry combined ratio: **114.8**. About four points attributable to **Hurricane Andrew**, the largest insured loss in history.

> "Andrew destroyed a few small insurers. Beyond that, it awakened some larger companies to the fact that their reinsurance protection against catastrophes was far from adequate. (It's only when the tide goes out that you learn who's been swimming naked.)"

> "One major insurer escaped insolvency solely because it had a wealthy parent that could promptly supply a massive transfusion of capital."

**And the near-miss:**
> "Andrew could easily have been far more damaging if it had hit Florida 20 or 30 miles north of where it actually did and had hit Louisiana further east."

**Berkshire's loss:** about **$125 million** — roughly equal to its entire 1992 super-cat premium income. Other super-cat losses were negligible, so the line produced an overall loss of only **$2 million**. Plus GEICO's net loss of ~$50M after reinsurance and tax, of which Berkshire's share is ~$25M (affecting look-through, not operating earnings).

**The 1991–92 aberration:** Both years came in near break-even, against his prediction of huge swings.
> "Nonetheless, I see these results as aberrations and stick with my prediction of huge annual swings in profitability from this business."

**The structure of super-cat policies** — explained in unusual detail:
- Triggered only when the policyholder suffers losses above a "retention" **and** industry-wide insured losses exceed a minimum, usually $3 billion+
- Often geographically limited
- Many are **second-event** (or third/fourth) policies
- Some are catastrophe-type specific (e.g., earthquake only)

> "Our exposures are large: We have one policy that calls for us to pay $100 million to the policyholder if a specified catastrophe occurs. (Now you know why I suffer eyestrain: from watching The Weather Channel.)"

**The capacity argument:**
> "Currently, Berkshire is second in the U.S. property-casualty industry in net worth (the leader being State Farm, which neither buys nor sells reinsurance). Therefore, we have the capacity to assume risk on a scale that interests virtually no other company."

**The discipline:**
> "The saying, 'a fool and his money are soon invited everywhere,' applies in spades in reinsurance, and we actually reject more than 98% of the business we are offered."

> "Ajit Jain, who runs our reinsurance operation, is simply the best in this business."

**Why pricing is hard** — three reasons:
1. Can't extrapolate past experience; "if there is truly 'global warming'... tiny changes in atmospheric conditions can produce momentous changes in weather patterns"
2. Mushrooming population and insured values in coastal areas: "A hurricane that caused x dollars of damage 20 years ago could easily cost 10x now"
3. The unthinkable happens — Charleston, S.C. 1886 (6.6), and **New Madrid, Missouri 1812 (estimated 8.7)** vs. San Francisco 1989 (7.1). "Each one-point Richter increase represents a ten-fold increase in strength."

> "Someday, a U.S. earthquake occurring far from California will cause enormous losses for insurers."

**The accounting quirk:**
> "Rather than recording our super-cat premiums on a pro-rata basis over the life of a given policy, we defer recognition of revenue until a loss occurs or until the policy expires."

Why: "Of the ten largest insured losses in U.S. history, nine occurred in the last half of the year."

> "The bottom-line effect... is this: Large losses may be reported in any quarter of the year, but significant profits will only be reported in the fourth quarter."

**The selling joke:**
> "Our pace of activity resembles that forced upon a traveler who found himself stuck in tiny Podunk's only hotel. With no T.V. in his room, he faced an evening of boredom. **The General Dynamics story** is the letter's best investment narrative. Buffett bought initially as an **arbitrage** — the company announced a Dutch tender for ~30% of its shares, and he expected to tender for a small profit. Then he studied the company and Bill Anders's record:

> "And what I saw made my eyes pop: Bill had a clearly articulated and rational strategy; he had been focused and imbued with a sense of urgency in carrying it out; and the results were truly remarkable."

> "In short order, I dumped my arbitrage thoughts and decided that Berkshire should become a long-term investor with Bill."

**The mechanics:** "a tender greatly swells the volume of trading in a stock. In a one-month period, we were able to purchase 14% of the General Dynamics shares that remained outstanding after the tender was completed."

## The Central Section: Value, Growth, and the Redundancy of "Value Investing"

This is the most important conceptual passage in the letter, and one of the most important in the entire corpus.

**The 1977 creed, restated:**
> "We select our marketable equity securities in much the way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety. We want the business to be one (a) that we can understand; (b) with favorable long-term prospects; (c) operated by honest and competent people; and (d) available at a very attractive price."

**The one change:**
> "Because of both market conditions and our size, we now substitute 'an attractive price' for 'a very attractive price.'"

**Then the demolition of the value/growth dichotomy:**

> "Most analysts feel they must choose between two approaches customarily thought to be in opposition: 'value' and 'growth.' Indeed, many investment professionals see any mixing of the two terms as a form of intellectual cross-dressing."

> "We view that as fuzzy thinking (in which, it must be confessed, I myself engaged some years ago). In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive."

> "In addition, we think the very term 'value investing' is redundant. What is 'investing' if it is not the act of seeking value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid? Consciously paying more for a stock than its calculated value — in the hope that it can soon be sold for a still-higher price — should be labeled speculation (which is neither illegal, immoral nor — in our view — financially fattening)."

**The critique of the conventional definition:**
> "Typically, it connotes the purchase of stocks having attributes such as a low ratio of price to book value, a low price-earnings ratio, or a high dividend yield. Unfortunately, such characteristics, even if they appear in combination, are far from determinative as to whether an investor is indeed buying something for what it is worth... Correspondingly, opposite characteristics — a high ratio of price to book value, a high price-earnings ratio, and a low dividend yield — are in no way inconsistent with a 'value' purchase."

**On growth, and the airline counterexample:**
> "Business growth, per se, tells us little about value. It's true that growth often has a positive impact on value, sometimes one of spectacular proportions. But such an effect is far from certain. For example, investors have regularly poured money into the domestic airline business to finance profitless (or worse) growth. For these investors, it would have been far better if Orville had failed to get off the ground at Kitty Hawk: The more the industry has grown, the worse the disaster for owners."

> "Growth benefits investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing — in other words, only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds, growth hurts the investor."

[until here]

**The John Burr Williams formulation:**
> "The value of any stock, bond or business today is determined by the cash inflows and outflows — discounted at an appropriate interest rate — that can be expected to occur during the remaining life of the asset."

**The crucial difference between bonds and stocks:**
> "A bond has a coupon and maturity date that define future cash flows; but in the case of equities, the investment analyst must himself estimate the future 'coupons.' Furthermore, the quality of management affects the bond coupon only rarely... In contrast, the ability of management can dramatically affect the equity 'coupons.'"

**The conclusion:**
> "The investment shown by the discounted-flows-of-cash calculation to be the cheapest is the one that the investor should purchase — irrespective of whether the business grows or doesn't, displays volatility or smoothness in its earnings, or carries a high price or low in relation to its current earnings and book value."

**The best and worst businesses:**
> "Leaving the question of price aside, the best business to own is one that over an extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return. The worst business to own is one that must, or will, do the opposite... Unfortunately, the first type of business is very hard to find: Most high-return businesses need relatively little capital."

**The two defenses against estimation error:**
1. **Stay inside your circle.** "If a business is complex or subject to constant change, we're not smart enough to predict future cash flows. Incidentally, that shortcoming doesn't bother us. What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know. An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes."
2. **Margin of safety.** "If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying. We believe this margin-of-safety principle, so strongly emphasized by Ben Graham, to be the cornerstone of investment success."

## Fixed Income: Why New Issues Are Inferior

The holdings: ACF debentures ($133M cost / $163M market), American Express Percs ($300M/$309M), Champion preferred ($300M/$309M), First Empire State preferred ($40M/$68M), Salomon preferred ($700M/$756M), USAir preferred ($358M/$268.5M), WPPSS bonds ($58.8M/$81M).

**The key insight** — why negotiated preferreds underperform secondary market purchases:

> "The secondary market, which is periodically ruled by mass folly, is constantly setting a 'clearing' price. No matter how foolish that price may be, it's what counts for the holder of a stock or bond who needs or wishes to sell, of whom there are always going to be a few at any moment. In many instances, shares worth x in business value have sold in the market for 1/2x or less."

> "The new-issue market, on the other hand, is ruled by controlling stockholders and corporations, who can usually select the timing of offerings or, if the market looks unfavorable, can avoid an offering altogether. Understandably, these sellers are not going to offer any bargains... It's rare you'll find x for 1/2x here. Indeed, in the case of common-stock offerings, selling shareholders are often motivated to unload only when they feel the market is overpaying."

That's a complete theory of why public offerings are structurally worse for buyers than open-market purchases. Sellers choose when to sell. Desperation isn't on the menu.

**The USAir confession:**
> "In the case of our commitment to USAir, industry economics had soured before the ink dried on our check. As I've previously mentioned, it was I who happily jumped into the pool; no one pushed me."

> "Yes, I knew the industry would be ruggedly competitive, but I did not expect its leaders to engage in prolonged kamikaze behavior. In the last two years, airline companies have acted as if they are members of a competitive tontine, which they wish to bring to its conclusion as rapidly as possible."

**The Seth Schofield praise, and the strike decision:**
> "He was particularly courageous in accepting a strike last fall that, had it been lengthy, might well have bankrupted the company. Capitulating to the striking union, however, would have been equally disastrous."

> "A competitively-beset business such as USAir requires far more managerial skill than does a business with fine economics. Unfortunately, though, the near-term reward for skill in the airline business is simply survival, not prosperity."

**The British Airways investment** — and Buffett and Munger joining the USAir board: "this makes five outside board memberships for me, which is more than I believe advisable for an active CEO."

> "We expect the managers of our investees to work hard to increase the value of the businesses they run, and there are times when large owners should do their bit as well."

## Accounting: Two Rules and a Plea

**Deferred taxes:** The 28% layer from pre-1987 gains must be restated at 34%, reducing net worth by **$70 million** in Q1 1993.

**Post-retirement health benefits:** The new rule forces recognition of the present-value liability.

> "In making acquisitions, Charlie and I have tended to avoid companies with significant post-retirement liabilities. As a result, Berkshire's present liability and future costs for post-retirement health benefits — though we now have 22,000 employees — are inconsequential."

**The 1982 near-miss:** He committed to buy a company burdened by extraordinary post-retirement health obligations; the deal fell through; the eventual buyer went bankrupt and thousands of workers found the promises "largely worthless."

**The systemic indictment:**
> "In recent decades, no CEO would have dreamed of going to his board with the proposition that his company become an insurer of uncapped post-retirement health benefits... A CEO didn't need to be a medical expert to know that lengthening life expectancies and soaring health costs would guarantee an insurer a financial battering from such a business. Nevertheless, many a manager blithely committed his own company to a self-insurance plan embodying precisely the same promises."

> "In health-care, open-ended promises have created open-ended liabilities that in a few cases loom so large as to threaten the global competitiveness of major American industries."

**Why it happened:** cash-basis accounting. "Out-of-sight, out-of-mind."

> "Ironically, some of these same managers would be quick to criticize Congress for employing 'cash-basis' thinking in respect to Social Security promises."

**Lincoln's riddle:**
> "How many legs does a dog have if you call his tail a leg?" The answer: "Four, because calling a tail a leg does not make it a leg."

> "It behooves managers to remember that Abe's right even if an auditor is willing to certify that the tail is a leg."

## The Stock Option Polemic

This is the most sustained and angry passage in the letter. It's also the intellectual foundation for everything he would argue about compensation for the next three decades.

**The industry's arguments, and their absurdity:**
- Options are hard to value, so ignore the cost
- Assigning a cost would injure small start-ups
- "Out-of-the-money" options have no value when issued
- The Council of Institutional Investors: options "aren't dollars out of a company's coffers"

**The offer:**
> "So if you're a CEO and subscribe to this 'no cash-no cost' theory of accounting, I'll make you an offer you can't refuse: Give us a call at Berkshire and we will happily sell you insurance in exchange for a bundle of long-term options on your company's stock."

**The principle:**
> "Shareholders should understand that companies incur costs when they deliver something of value to another party and not just when cash changes hands. Moreover, it is both silly and cynical to say that an important item of cost should not be recognized simply because it can't be quantified with pinpoint precision."

**The comparison:** no one knows how long a 747 lasts, what a bank's loan loss charge should be, or what P/C loss reserves will prove to be — and all of those are estimated and recorded.

**The second offer:**
> "On the day of issue, Berkshire will pay him or her a substantial sum for the right to any future gain he or she realizes on the option. So if you find a CEO who says his newly-issued options have little or no value, tell him to try us out. In truth, we have far more confidence in our ability to determine an appropriate price to pay for an option than we have in our ability to determine the proper depreciation rate for our corporate jet."

**The summary:**
> "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"

**The institutional indictment:**
> "The accounting profession and the SEC should be shamed by the fact that they have long let themselves be muscled by business executives on the option-accounting issue. Additionally, the lobbying that executives engage in may have an unfortunate by-product: In my opinion, the business elite risks losing its credibility on issues of significance to society — about which it may have much of value to say — when it advocates the incredible on issues of significance to itself."

## Miscellaneous

**Retirements:** Gladys Kaiser gives up her assistant role after 25 years. Verne McKenzie steps down as CFO after 30 years; Marc Hamburg succeeds him. The archaeologist joke: "The older I get, the more he'll be interested in me."

**Mrs. B at 99:** She left NFM in 1989 after a managerial disagreement and opened her own operation next door. At the end of 1992 she **sold her building and land to NFM** and will continue running her carpet business there while NFM sets up alongside.

> "This time around, Mrs. B graciously offered to sign a non-compete agreement — and I, having been incautious on this point when she was 89, snapped at the deal. Mrs. B belongs in the Guinness Book of World Records on many counts. Signing a non-compete at 99 merely adds one more."

**Ralph Schey / Scott Fetzer:** record **$110 million pre-tax** on only **$116 million of equity capital**, with minimal leverage.

> "Scott Fetzer now operates with a significantly smaller investment in both inventory and fixed assets than it had when we bought it in 1986. This means the company has been able to distribute more than 100% of its earnings to Berkshire during our seven years of ownership while concurrently increasing its earnings stream."

That's the See's pattern generalized: a business that shrinks its capital base while growing its earnings.

**The jet:** "Forget it! I find the thought of retiring the plane even more revolting than the thought of retiring the Chairman. (In this matter I've demonstrated uncharacteristic flexibility: For years I argued passionately against corporate jets. But finally my dogma was run over by my karma.)"

**Overhead:** After-tax corporate costs under **1% of reported operating earnings** and **less than 0.5% of look-through earnings**. No legal, personnel, PR, investor relations, or strategic planning departments. "Professor Parkinson would like our operation — though Charlie, I must say, still finds it outrageously fat."

**The capital-value point:**
> "If the business that spends 10% on headquarters' costs achieves earnings at its operating levels identical to those achieved by the business that incurs costs of only 1%, shareholders of the first enterprise suffer a 9% loss in the value of their holdings simply because of corporate overhead."

**Admired models:** Wal-Mart, Nucor, Dover, GEICO, Golden West Financial, Price Co.

**No stock split:** Reaffirms the 1983 position. "There is no way that our shareholder group would be upgraded by the new shareholders enticed by a split. Instead we believe that modest degradation would occur."

**Gift-giving advice for the $10,000 threshold:** (1) married couples can give $20,000 with spousal consent; (2) bargain sale — sell a $12,000 share for $2,000; (3) fund a partnership with shares and give percentage interests. With the caveat to consult a tax advisor.

**Debenture call:** The 5.5% zeros called for January 4, 1993. "A low cost for funds when they were issued in 1989, but an unattractive rate for us at the time of call."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1992 Edition)

**1. He refuses the value/growth dichotomy on principle.** This is the most important intellectual move in the letter. Growth isn't a separate style — it's a variable inside the DCF equation whose sign can be positive or negative. Airlines are his proof that growth can destroy value.

**2. He measures against a pre-tax index and admits it's unfair.** Point 2 of the volatility section is a rare, explicit acknowledgment that Berkshire's reported results are structurally handicapped relative to the S&P because of corporate taxation.

**3. He defines what he doesn't know, precisely.** "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know." That's the circle of competence doctrine stated as an epistemic discipline rather than a slogan.

**4. He pre-empts the "long-term" excuse.** The Alice/"jam tomorrow" line is aimed at himself as much as anyone. You can't claim a long-term focus forever without delivering — and he commits to a specific number ($1.8 billion by 2000) to be judged against.

**5. He treats accounting as a moral issue, not a technical one.** The Lincoln dog-legs riddle, the dog and tail, the health-benefit indictment, the option polemic — all of it frames accounting choices as choices about honesty. "Calling a tail a leg does not make it a leg."

**6. He explains why sellers' incentives determine the price you get.** The secondary-market vs. new-issue comparison is a complete theory of why you should prefer the auction to the negotiation. The seller in a new issue chooses the timing; the seller in the secondary market is often forced.

**7. He is willing to change his mind mid-transaction.** The General Dynamics story is a model of intellectual honesty: he entered as an arbitrageur, studied the business, and converted to a long-term owner. Most investors would have taken the tender profit and moved on.

**8. He measures success in slugging percentage.** "A tolerance for short-term swings improves our long-term prospects." The willingness to look bad in any given quarter is the price of the lumpy outcome.

## The Key Quote

> "In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive."

This is the definitive statement. It's what separates Buffett's framework from both the academic orthodoxy (which treats growth and value as separate factors) and from the self-described "value investors" who screen for low P/E and low P/B. Value is not a set of ratios. It's a discounted cash flow calculation, and growth is one of its inputs — sometimes helpful, sometimes harmful.

But the passage that best captures the *spirit* of the letter — the thing that makes it more than a collection of good arguments — is this:

> "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know. An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes."

The whole architecture follows from that. The circle of competence. The margin of safety. The refusal to buy airlines at scale. The avoidance of post-retirement health liabilities. The 98% rejection rate in reinsurance. The preference for the secondary market over new issues. Every one of those is an application of the same epistemic humility: *I don't need to be brilliant; I need to know where I'm ignorant and stay away from it.*
