# Analysis of the 1988 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 28, 1989)

## The Performance: Another Strong Year

Net worth gain: **$569 million**, or **20.0%**. Over 24 years, book value per share grew from $19.46 to **$2,974.52** – **23.0% compounded annually**.

But Buffett immediately shifts focus to intrinsic business value, noting it significantly exceeds book value. In 1988, book value grew slightly faster than business value, a reversal from most years.

## The Growing Capital Base Problem

Buffett confronts the mathematical reality of size:

> "Four years ago I told you that we needed profits of $3.9 billion to achieve a 15% annual return over the decade then ahead. Today, for the next decade, a 15% return demands profits of $10.3 billion. That seems like a very big number to me and to Charlie Munger."

He adds a wry note: "Should that number indeed prove too big, Charlie will find himself, in future reports, retrospectively identified as the senior partner."

Four negative factors affecting prospects:
1. A less attractive stock market than generally existed over the past 24 years
2. Higher corporate tax rates on most forms of investment income
3. A far more richly-priced market for acquisitions
4. Industry conditions for the three permanent investments (Cap Cities, GEICO, Washington Post) ranging from slightly to materially less favorable

## The Sainted Seven: 67% Return on Equity

The seven non-financial units earned **67% on average equity capital** in 1988, up from 57% in 1987. "In most cases the remarkable performance of these units arises partially from an exceptional business franchise; in all cases an exceptional management is a vital factor. The contribution Charlie and I make is to leave these managers alone."

## Borsheim's: The Family Connection

Buffett recounts the purchase of 80% of Borsheim's, an Omaha jewelry store. The connection: Rose Blumkin (Mrs. B of Nebraska Furniture Mart) came from Russia in 1917; her sister Rebecca Friedman followed in 1922. Rebecca's husband Louis bought Borsheim's in 1948. The families share the same creed:

> "Sell cheap and tell the truth."

The purchase was made without audited financial statements, inventory verification, or receivables audit. "Ike simply told us what was so – and on that basis we drew up a one-page contract and wrote a large check."

Buffett's key insight: "Most people, no matter how sophisticated they are in other matters, feel like babes in the woods when purchasing jewelry. They can judge neither quality nor price. For them only one rule makes sense: If you don't know jewelry, know the jeweler."

## Insurance: Industry Deterioration and Proposition 103

The industry combined ratio worsened to 105.4 in 1988 (estimated). Buffett predicts the underwriting loss will continue and accelerate for at least two more years.

He contrasts the public's attitude toward auto insurance versus breakfast cereal:

> "The cereal companies regularly impose price increases, few of them related to a significant jump in their costs. Yet not a peep is heard from consumers. But when auto insurers raise prices by amounts that do not even match cost increases, customers are outraged. If you want to be loved, it's clearly better to sell high-priced corn flakes than low-priced auto insurance."

**Proposition 103** in California threatens to push auto insurance prices below cost. Buffett warns: "If voters insist that auto insurance be priced below cost, it eventually must be sold by government. Stockholders can subsidize policyholders for a short period, but only taxpayers can subsidize them over the long term."

This threatens GEICO specifically because it is a low-cost producer with huge economic goodwill at risk. "At most property-casualty companies, socialized auto insurance would be no disaster... But GEICO, because it is a low-cost producer able to earn high returns on equity, has a huge amount of economic goodwill at risk. In turn, so do we."

## Marketable Securities: The Coca-Cola Purchase

This is the letter where Buffett announces the Coca-Cola purchase: **14,172,500 shares** at a cost of **$592.5 million**, market value $632.4 million at yearend. He also purchased **Freddie Mac preferred** (2,400,000 shares at cost of $71.7 million, market $121.2 million).

The permanent holdings (Cap Cities, GEICO, Washington Post) remain unchanged.

Buffett articulates his philosophy:

> "When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever. We are just the opposite of those who hurry to sell and book profits when companies perform well but who tenaciously hang on to businesses that disappoint. Peter Lynch aptly likens such behavior to cutting the flowers and watering the weeds."

He adds: "We agree with Mae West: 'Too much of a good thing can be wonderful.'"

## The CEO Problem: Why Inadequate Managers Keep Their Jobs

Buffett includes a brilliant passage on why CEOs are rarely fired for poor performance:

- Secretaries and salespeople are easily measured and quickly let go if they fail.
- CEOs have fuzzy performance standards, no immediate superior whose own performance is measured, and boards that seldom measure themselves.
- "At board meetings, criticism of the CEO's performance is often viewed as the social equivalent of belching."

He concludes: "Most are able and hard-working, and a number are truly outstanding. But the management failings that Charlie and I have seen make us thankful that we are linked with the managers of our three permanent holdings."

## Arbitrage: The Arcata Story and EMT Critique

Buffett provides a detailed case study of Arcata Corp., an arbitrage involving a redwood timber claim. He explains the four questions to ask:
1. How likely is the promised event to occur?
2. How long will money be tied up?
3. What chance is there for something better?
4. What happens if the event doesn't occur?

The Arcata deal: bought at ~$33.50, received $37.50 plus additional $29.48 per share from the redwood settlement, for a 15% annual return excluding the redwood claim, and much more including it.

**Efficient Market Theory critique:** Buffett uses the 63-year arbitrage record of Graham-Newman, Buffett Partnership, and Berkshire to debunk EMT:

> "Over the 63 years, the general market delivered just under a 10% annual return, including dividends. That means $1,000 would have grown to $405,000 if all income had been reinvested. A 20% rate of return, however, would have produced $97 million. That strikes us as a statistically-significant differential that might, conceivably, arouse one's curiosity."

He adds: "From a selfish point of view, Grahamites should probably endow chairs to ensure the perpetual teaching of EMT."

## New York Stock Exchange Listing

Berkshire listed on the NYSE on November 29, 1988. Buffett explains the goal was to reduce transaction costs (the spread between bid and asked narrowed). But he emphasizes two differences from most listed companies:

1. **They don't want to maximize the share price.** They want it to trade in a narrow range centered at intrinsic business value.
2. **They want very little trading activity.** "If we ran a private business with a few passive partners, we would be disappointed if those partners... frequently wanted to leave the partnership."

He adds: "We don't understand the CEO who wants lots of stock activity, for that can be achieved only if many of his owners are constantly exiting. At what other organization – school, club, church, etc. – do leaders cheer when members leave?"

## David L. Dodd Tribute

Buffett honors Dave Dodd, co-author of *Security Analysis* with Ben Graham, who died at 93. "Any long-time shareholder of Berkshire is appreciably wealthier because of the indirect influence he had upon our company." He notes Dodd's financial performance far outshone Keynes, who began as a market-timer and converted to value investing. "Dave was right from the start."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1988 Edition)

**1. He confronts the math of size honestly.**
The $10.3 billion profit requirement for 15% returns over the next decade is a sobering admission. He doesn't pretend the past can be repeated.

**2. He doubles down on permanent holdings.**
The Coca-Cola purchase is a major commitment to a single company. He intends to hold forever. This is the opposite of trading.

**3. He values family businesses and handshake deals.**
Borsheim's was purchased without audit or inventory verification, based solely on trust in Ike Friedman. "Ike simply told us what was so."

**4. He sees the structural problems of corporate governance.**
His analysis of why CEOs keep their jobs is one of his sharpest critiques of corporate boards.

**5. He debunks efficient market theory with data.**
The 63-year arbitrage record is his strongest empirical rebuttal to EMT.

**6. He thinks in terms of business value, not market price.**
For the NYSE listing, he explicitly says they don't want to maximize share price – they want it centered on intrinsic value.

**7. He honors his teachers.**
The tribute to Dave Dodd is moving: "Our prosperity is the fruit of their intellectual tree."

## The Key Quote

> "When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever."

This is the essence of Buffett's mature investment philosophy. He has moved beyond arbitrage and short-term trading to permanent ownership of great businesses. The Coca-Cola purchase embodies this. The permanent holdings (Cap Cities, GEICO, Washington Post) already did. Now Coca-Cola joins them.

But the quote that best captures his view of management and governance:

> "The supreme irony of business management is that it is far easier for an inadequate CEO to keep his job than it is for an inadequate subordinate."

That is a profound observation about the structural flaws of corporate governance. Buffett has seen it up close, and it makes him grateful for the managers he has.

## Transition Note

1988 was a pivotal year. Berkshire listed on the NYSE. Buffett made his first major purchase of Coca-Cola, a stock he would hold for decades. Borsheim's joined the family. The arbitrage discussion provided a definitive rebuttal to efficient market theory. And the tribute to Dave Dodd reminded shareholders of the intellectual foundations of Buffett's approach.

The letter is also notable for what it doesn't say: There's no lengthy discussion of textile problems (they're gone), no major new insurance initiatives, and a clear-eyed acceptance that future returns will be lower. Buffett is preparing shareholders for a different era. The easy days are over. But the machine he has built – the Sainted Seven, the permanent holdings, the insurance float – is a remarkable engine for compounding. The question is whether he can deploy the capital effectively.
