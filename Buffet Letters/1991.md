# Analysis of the 1991 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 28, 1992)

## The Performance: 39.6%, But Nearly All of It From Two Stocks

Net worth gain: **$2.1 billion**, or **39.6%**. Over 27 years: $19.46 → **$6,437** per share, **23.7% compounded annually**.

But Buffett immediately flags the composition:

> "Our outsized gain in book value in 1991 resulted from a phenomenon not apt to be repeated: a dramatic rise in the price-earnings ratios of Coca-Cola and Gillette. These two stocks accounted for nearly $1.6 billion of our $2.1 billion growth in net worth last year."

And the scale shift:

> "When we loaded up on Coke three years ago, Berkshire's net worth was $3.4 billion; now our Coke stock alone is worth more than that."

**The double-dip, again:** "We got a double-dip benefit, delivered partly by the excellent earnings growth and even more so by the market's reappraisal of these stocks... But it can't recur annually: We'll have to settle for a single dip in the future."

**The size arithmetic, now explicit:**
- Equity capital: **$7.4 billion**
- At $20 million of capital, a $1 million idea added 5 percentage points to the return
- Now, achieving the same requires a **$370 million idea** (over $550 million pre-tax)
- "And there are many more ways to make $1 million than to make $370 million"
- To grow intrinsic value 15% over the next decade requires earning **$22 billion**. "Wish us luck — we'll need it."

## A Second Job: Salomon

On August 18, 1991, Buffett was elected Interim Chairman of Salomon Inc.

> "In 1989 when I — a happy consumer of five cans of Cherry Coke daily — announced our purchase of $1 billion worth of Coca-Cola stock, I described the move as a rather extreme example of putting our money where my mouth was. On August 18 of last year... it was a different story: I put my mouth where our money was."

The implicit message: the operating managers are so good that he could step away without Berkshire's progress skipping a beat.

**The George Mira story:** Miami's quarterback dropped back to pass, found his right shoulder in the grip of a Florida linebacker, switched the ball to his left hand, and threw "the only left-handed pass of his life — for a touchdown." Coach Andy Gustafson turned to a reporter: **"Now that's what I call coaching."**

> "Given the managerial stars we have at our operating units, Berkshire's performance is not affected if Charlie or I slip away from time to time."

On the "interim" title: at Harvard Business School, a student asked when he planned to retire. **"About five to ten years after I die."**

## Look-Through Earnings: The First Decline

This is the most important financial section. Look-through earnings **fell 14%** — from $602M to $516M. Buffett names four causes, two of which he'd predicted and two he hadn't:

1. **Media earnings declined** — he'd warned they were "sure to decline"
2. **Gillette preferred conversion on April 1** — 1990 after-tax preferred earnings were ~$45M; 1991 combined only three months of preferred dividends plus nine months of common look-through
3. **Wells Fargo broke even** — dividends offset by negative retained earnings. "Last year I said that such a result at Wells was 'a low-level possibility — not a likelihood.'"
4. **Lower insurance profits** — "still excellent"

**The table:**

| Investee | Ownership | Share of Undistributed Earnings |
|---|---|---|
| Cap Cities/ABC | 18.1% | $61M (from $85M) |
| Coca-Cola | 7.0% | $69M (from $58M) |
| Freddie Mac | 3.4% | $15M |
| Gillette | 11.0% | $23M (9 months) |
| GEICO | 48.2% | $69M (from $76M) |
| Washington Post | 14.6% | $10M (from $18M) |
| Wells Fargo | 9.6% | **($17M)** (from $19M) |

**The historical anchor:** "Since present management took over in 1965, our look-through earnings have grown at almost the identical 23% rate of gain recorded for book value." The 1991 decline is the first break in that record.

**The investor's version of the exercise** — this is a rare piece of direct advice:

> "We also believe that investors can benefit by focusing on their own look-through earnings... The goal of each investor should be to create a portfolio (in effect, a 'company') that will deliver him or her the highest possible look-through earnings a decade or so from now."

> "An approach of this kind will force the investor to think about long-term business prospects rather than short-term stock market prospects... In investing, just as in baseball, to put runs on the scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."

## The Media Section: Franchise vs. Business

This is the letter's central intellectual contribution — arguably the most important conceptual passage in the entire corpus after the 1983 Goodwill appendix.

**The definition of a franchise:**
> "An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital. Moreover, franchises can tolerate mis-management. Inept managers may diminish a franchise's profitability, but they cannot inflict mortal damage."

**The definition of "a business":**
> "In contrast, 'a business' earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long. With superior management, a company may maintain its status as a low-cost operator for a much longer time, but even then unceasingly faces the possibility of competitive attack. And a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management."

**The diagnosis of media:**
> "Until recently, media properties possessed the three characteristics of a franchise... Now, however, consumers looking for information and entertainment... enjoy greatly broadened choices as to where to find them. Unfortunately, demand can't expand in response to this new supply: 500 million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day are all that's available."

**The valuation math** — this is the killer insight:

Under the old assumption (6% perpetual growth, no incremental capital, 10% discount rate):
- $1 million of after-tax earnings → **$25 million valuation** (25x after-tax, ~16x pre-tax)

Under the new assumption (earnings bob around $1 million cyclically):
- $1 million of earnings → **$10 million valuation** (10x after-tax, ~6.5x pre-tax)

> "Thus a seemingly modest shift in assumptions reduces the property's valuation to 10 times after-tax earnings... Dollars are dollars whether they are derived from the operation of media properties or of steel mills. What in the past caused buyers to value a dollar of earnings from media far higher than a dollar from steel was that the earnings of a media property were expected to constantly grow (without the business requiring much additional capital), whereas steel earnings clearly fell in the bob-around category."

**This is the lesson:** a change in growth expectations doesn't produce a proportional change in value — it produces a *discontinuous* collapse, because the asset reclassifies from annuity to cyclical. That's why media values fell so far, so fast.

**The honest assessment of Berkshire's exposure:** intrinsic value of the media investment "has declined materially." Cyclical factors hurt current earnings but don't reduce intrinsic value; the secular transformation does.

**Why they don't sell:** per the Business Principles, they don't sell permanent holdings "simply because we see ways to use the money more advantageously elsewhere."

**Why the damage was moderated:** Buffalo News outperformed most newspapers; Cap Cities and Washington Post are exceptionally well-managed, stayed out of the late-1980s irrational acquisition bidding, carry little debt roughly offset by cash, and are "about the only" major public media companies essentially debt-free. Most peers have debt at five-plus times current net income.

> "But gone are the days of bullet-proof franchises and cornucopian economics."

## Twenty Years in a Candy Store: The See's Retrospective

The 20th anniversary of the January 3, 1972 purchase. This is the letter's emotional and pedagogical centerpiece.

**The purchase:** Sellers asked $40 million; the company had $10 million of excess cash, so the true price was $30 million. Buffett and Munger, "not yet fully appreciative of the value of an economic franchise," looked at $7 million of tangible net worth and offered $25 million — "and we meant it." The sellers accepted.

**The results over 20 years:**

| Metric | 1972 | 1991 |
|---|---|---|
| See's sales | $29M | $196M |
| See's pre-tax profits | $4.2M | $42.4M |
| Blue Chip stamp sales | $102.5M | $1.2M |

**The capital-efficiency point — the heart of it:**
> "See's has been astounding: The company now operates comfortably with only $25 million of net worth, which means that our beginning base of $7 million has had to be supplemented by only $18 million of reinvested earnings. Meanwhile, See's remaining pre-tax profits of $410 million were distributed to Blue Chip/Berkshire during the 20 years for these companies to deploy... in whatever way made most sense."

So: $25 million purchase price, $410 million distributed, $18 million reinvested. That's the economic goodwill engine in one paragraph.

**The one insight and the two pieces of luck:**
> "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the business had untapped pricing power. Otherwise, we were lucky twice over. First, the transaction was not derailed by our dumb insistence on a $25 million price. Second, we found Chuck Huggins."

**The handshake:** "When the purchase was made, we shook hands with Chuck on a compensation arrangement — conceived in about five minutes and never reduced to a written contract — that remains unchanged to this day."

**1991 performance:** dollar sales flat, pounds down 4% (all in the last two months, which normally produce 80%+ of annual profits), profits up 7%, pre-tax margin a record **21.6%**. Hurt by the California recession and by a new California sales tax on "snack food."

**The epistemological comedy** — the taxable/non-taxable table:

| Taxable "Snack" | Non-Taxable "Non-Snack" |
|---|---|
| Ritz Crackers | Soda Crackers |
| Popped Popcorn | Unpopped Popcorn |
| Granola Bars | Granola Cereal |
| Slice of Pie (Wrapped) | Whole Pie |
| Milky Way Candy Bar | Milky Way Ice Cream Bar |

> "What — you are sure to ask — is the tax status of a melted Milky Way ice cream bar? In that androgynous form, does it more resemble an ice cream bar or a candy bar that has been left in the sun?"

Brad Sherman, Chairman of the State Board of Equalization: **"I came to this job as a specialist in tax law. Now I find my constituents should have elected Julia Child."**

**The payoff:**
> "Ownership of See's has taught us much about the evaluation of franchises. We've made significant money in certain common stocks because of the lessons we learned at See's."

That's the thesis of the whole letter in two sentences. See's was the tuition; Coke and Gillette were the returns.

## H.H. Brown: The Acquisition and the Compensation System

**The history:** Ray Heffernan bought the company in 1927 for $10,000 at age 29 and ran it for 62 years. At 90 he was "still joining new golf clubs." By his 1990 retirement: three U.S. plants, one Canadian, ~2,000 employees, ~$25 million pre-tax.

**The family twist:** Heffernan warned his daughter Frances's fiancé, Frank Rooney, to forget any ideas about working for his father-in-law. "That was one of Mr. Heffernan's few mistakes." Rooney became CEO of Melville Shoe, and over 23 years (1964–1986) Melville's earnings averaged **over 20% on equity** and the stock rose from **$16 to $960** (split-adjusted).

**The lucky connection:** After Heffernan died, the family hired a major investment banker — who failed to think of Berkshire. Then Frank Rooney played golf in Florida with **John Loomis**, a longtime Berkshire shareholder "always on the alert for something that might fit us." Loomis told Rooney to call Buffett. Deal done.

**Why Buffett was enthusiastic:** Rooney's willingness to continue as CEO.
> "Like most of our managers, he has no financial need to work but does so because he loves the game and likes to excel. Managers of this stripe cannot be 'hired' in the normal sense of the word. What we must do is provide a concert hall in which business artists of this class will wish to perform."

**The business:** Leading North American manufacturer of work shoes and boots. "Shoes are a tough business — of the billion pairs purchased in the United States each year, about 85% are imported — and most manufacturers in the industry do poorly." Wide style/size range means heavy inventories and substantial receivables.

**The compensation system** — this is the section that matters most conceptually:

> "A number of key managers are paid an annual salary of $7,800, to which is added a designated percentage of the profits of the company after these are reduced by a charge for capital employed. These managers therefore truly stand in the shoes of owners."

> "In contrast, most managers talk the talk but don't walk the walk, choosing instead to employ compensation systems that are long on carrots but short on sticks (and that almost invariably treat equity capital as if it were cost-free)."

> "Managers eager to bet heavily on their abilities usually have plenty of ability to bet on."

**The intermediary indictment:**
> "It's discouraging to note that though we have on four occasions made major purchases of companies whose sellers were represented by prominent investment banks, we were in only one of these instances contacted by the investment bank."

## Insurance: The Float Table and Super-Cats

**1991 was a bad underwriting year:** pre-tax underwriting loss of **$119.6 million** vs. $26.6 million in 1990. After-tax: ($77.2M) vs. ($14.9M).

**The float cost table — updated through 1991:**

| Year | Underwriting Loss | Average Float | Cost of Funds | Govt Bond Yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1987 | $55.43M | $1,266.7M | 4.38% | 8.95% |
| 1988 | $11.08M | $1,497.7M | 0.74% | 9.00% |
| 1989 | $24.40M | $1,541.3M | 1.58% | 7.97% |
| 1990 | $26.65M | $1,637.3M | 1.63% | 8.24% |
| **1991** | **$119.6M** | **$1,895.0M** | **6.31%** | **7.40%** |

> "As you can see, our cost of funds in 1991 was well below the U.S. Government's cost on newly-issued long-term bonds. We have in fact beat the government's rate in 20 of the 25 years we have been in the insurance business, often by a wide margin."

**The industry combined ratio problem:** premiums grew only 3.1% (vs. the ~10% needed) yet the combined ratio *improved* slightly to 109.1. Buffett is skeptical:

> "Loss-reserve data for the industry indicate that there is reason to be skeptical of that outcome... In the long run, of course, trouble awaits managements that paper over operating problems with accounting maneuvers. Eventually, managements of this kind achieve the same result as the seriously-ill patient who tells his doctor: 'I can't afford the operation, but would you accept a small payment to touch up the x-rays?'"

**Super-cats, restated:**
> "We price this business expecting to pay out, over the long term, about 90% of the premiums we receive. In any given year, however, we are likely to appear either enormously profitable or enormously unprofitable... In effect, a one-year accounting cycle is ill-suited to the nature of this business."

**The 1991 surprise:** The biggest catastrophe wasn't the Oakland fire or Hurricane Bob — it was a **September typhoon in Japan**, with insured losses estimated at $4–5 billion, potentially exceeding Hugo's record.

**The cycle prediction:**
> "In the 1980's much of this reinsurance was supplied by 'innocents' — that is, by insurers that did not understand the risks of the business — but they have now been financially burned beyond recognition. (Berkshire itself was an innocent all too often when I was personally running the insurance operation.) Insurers, though, like investors, eventually repeat their mistakes. At some point — probably after a few catastrophe-scarce years — innocents will reappear and prices for super-cat policies will plunge to silly levels."

**The competitive advantage, stated with a Jackie Mason line:**
> "Thinking insurers know that when 'the big one' comes, many reinsurers who found it easy to write policies will find it difficult to write checks. (Some reinsurers can say what Jackie Mason does: 'I'm fixed for life — as long as I don't buy anything.')"

The portfolio at yearend:

| Company | Shares | Cost (000s) | Market (000s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cap Cities/ABC | 3,000,000 | $517,500 | $1,300,500 |
| Coca-Cola | 46,700,000 | $1,023,920 | $3,747,675 |
| Freddie Mac | 2,495,200 | $77,245 | $343,090 |
| GEICO | 6,850,000 | $45,713 | $1,363,150 |
| Gillette | 24,000,000 | $600,000 | $1,347,000 |
| **Guinness PLC** | **31,247,000** | **$264,782** | **$296,755** |
| Washington Post | 1,727,765 | $9,731 | $336,050 |
| Wells Fargo | 5,000,000 | $289,431 | $290,000 |

**Guinness is the first significant non-U.S. investment.** But the framing is instructive: "Guinness, however, earns its money in much the same fashion as Coca-Cola and Gillette, U.S.-based companies that garner most of their profits from international operations... in the sense of where they earn their profits — continent-by-continent — Coca-Cola and Guinness display strong similarities. (But you'll never get their drinks confused — and your Chairman remains unmovably in the Cherry Coke camp.)"

**The relocation-center line:**
> "Our stay-put behavior reflects our view that the stock market serves as a relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient. (With tongue only partly in check, I suggest that recent events indicate that the much-maligned 'idle rich' have received a bad rap: They have maintained or increased their wealth while many of the 'energetic rich' — aggressive real estate operators, corporate acquirers, oil drillers, etc. — have seen their fortunes disappear.)"

**The concentration argument, in its clearest form:**

> "If my universe of business possibilities was limited, say, to private companies in Omaha, I would, first, try to assess the long-term economic characteristics of each business; second, assess the quality of the people in charge of running it; and, third, try to buy into a few of the best operations at a sensible price. I certainly would not wish to own an equal part of every business in town. Why, then, should Berkshire take a different tack when dealing with the larger universe of public companies?"

> "And since finding great businesses and outstanding managers is so difficult, why should we discard proven products? (I was tempted to say 'the real thing.') Our motto is: 'If at first you do succeed, quit trying.'"

**The Keynes letter** — August 15, 1934, to F.C. Scott — is quoted at length, and it's the intellectual ancestor of everything above:
> "As time goes on, I get more and more convinced that the right method in investment is to put fairly large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about and in the management of which one thoroughly believes. It is a mistake to think that one limits one's risk by spreading too much between enterprises about which one knows little and has no reason for special confidence... One's knowledge and experience are definitely limited and there are seldom more than two or three enterprises at any given time in which I personally feel myself entitled to put full confidence."

## Mistake Du Jour: Fannie Mae

A new recurring feature — and the first entry is a doozy.

**The setup:** Early 1988, Berkshire decided to buy **30 million shares** (split-adjusted) of Fannie Mae — a **$350–400 million** investment. They'd owned it before and understood the business. David Maxwell, the CEO, had dealt superbly with inherited problems. Buffett visited him in Washington and confirmed he'd be comfortable with a large position.

**What happened:** They bought about **7 million shares**. The price began to climb. "In frustration, I stopped buying." Then:

> "In an even sillier move, I surrendered to my distaste for holding small positions and sold the 7 million shares we owned."

**The cost:** "As of yearend 1991... about **$1.4 billion**."

**The honesty:**
> "I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation for my amateurish behavior vis-a-vis Fannie Mae. But there isn't one."

**Why it's filed under "omission":**
> "Typically, our most egregious mistakes fall in the omission, rather than the commission, category. That may spare Charlie and me some embarrassment, since you don't see these errors; but their invisibility does not reduce their cost."

And he explicitly excludes the excuses: this wasn't Xerox, Apple, or Wal-Mart — businesses beyond their competence. Fannie Mae was "a business situation that Charlie and I can understand and that seems clearly attractive."

**The meta-point:** He notes he did *not* repeat the error with Coca-Cola when its price rose during the purchase program. That's the lesson: the behavioral error was stopping, and the secondary error was selling a small position out of aesthetic distaste rather than economics.

**First Empire State:** $40M, 9% coupon, non-callable until 1996, convertible at $78.91. "Normally I would think a purchase of this size too small for Berkshire, but I have enormous respect for Bob Wilmers, CEO of First Empire, and like being his partner on any scale."

**American Express Perc:** 8.85% on $300M; must convert three years after issuance into a maximum of 12,244,898 shares; the conversion ratio adjusts downward to cap total value at **$414 million** — "there is thus a ceiling on the value of the common stock that we will receive upon conversion, [but] there is no floor." One-year extension if the common is below $24.50 at the third anniversary.

**The USAir admission:**
> "Last year I told you that our USAir investment 'should work out all right unless the industry is decimated during the next few years.' Unfortunately 1991 was a decimating period for the industry, as Midway, Pan Am and America West all entered bankruptcy. (Stretch the period to 14 months and you can add Continental and TWA.)"

**The bankrupt-carrier dynamic:**
> "These carriers can temporarily charge fares that are below the industry's costs because the bankrupts don't incur the capital costs faced by their solvent brethren and because they can fund their losses — and thereby stave off shutdown — by selling off assets. This burn-the-furniture-to-provide-firewood approach to fare-setting by bankrupt carriers contributes to the toppling of previously-marginal carriers, creating a domino effect that is perfectly designed to bring the industry to its knees."

**The industry's aggregate record:** "Despite the huge amounts of equity capital that have been injected into it, the industry, in aggregate, has posted a net loss since its birth after Kitty Hawk."

**Salomon:** "As I write this letter, I am also writing a letter for inclusion in Salomon's annual report." Despite the travails, "Charlie and I believe our Salomon preferred stock increased slightly in value during 1991."

**Total:** $152 million of capital gains from fixed-income holdings in 1991.

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1991 Edition)

**1. He distinguishes franchises from businesses — and knows the distinction is about durability, not quality.** A franchise tolerates bad management; a business can be killed by it. Media used to be the former and is now the latter. That single reclassification explains the valuation collapse.

**2. He understands that expectation changes produce discontinuous valuation changes.** The 25x → 10x math is the clearest demonstration he ever gave that price is a function of *assumed* growth, and that when the assumption breaks, the price doesn't fall proportionally — it falls categorically.

**3. He measures the holding company by look-through earnings, not reported earnings.** And he extends the same discipline to individual investors: build a portfolio that will deliver the highest look-through earnings a decade out. "Watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."

**4. He treats See's as tuition, not just an asset.** "Ownership of See's has taught us much about the evaluation of franchises. We've made significant money in certain common stocks because of the lessons we learned at See's." The purchase was the education; Coke and Gillette were the application.

**5. He believes compensation should charge for capital.** H.H. Brown's $7,800 salary plus a share of profits after a capital charge is his ideal. Most option plans, by contrast, "almost invariably treat equity capital as if it were cost-free."

**6. He names his omission errors because they're the expensive ones.** The Fannie Mae confession ($1.4 billion) is more useful than any success story, because it isolates the behavioral failure: stopping when the price rose, then selling a small position for aesthetic reasons.

**7. He treats the stock market as a service, not an oracle.** "A relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient."

**8. He is willing to say a thesis was wrong.** The media franchise is broken. The intrinsic value has declined materially. He doesn't dress it up as cyclical.

## The Key Quote

> "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the business had untapped pricing power."

That's the whole letter. See's taught them what a franchise is. The media section is the same lesson in reverse — what happens when pricing power erodes. The H.H. Brown compensation system is the same lesson applied to management incentives. The Fannie Mae mistake is what happens when you fail to act on a franchise you've already identified.

But the most important passage for investors — the one that reframes the entire exercise — is this:

> "The goal of each investor should be to create a portfolio (in effect, a 'company') that will deliver him or her the highest possible look-through earnings a decade or so from now."

Not the highest price next year. Not the best relative performance this quarter. The highest *look-through earnings a decade out*. That's the scoreboard that matters, and it's the one almost nobody keeps.
