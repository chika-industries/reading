# Analysis of Charlie Munger's 1997 Wesco Financial Letter (March 13, 1998)

This is a rare treat: Munger writing directly to shareholders in his own voice, without Buffett's editorial hand. The contrast with the annual Berkshire letters is instructive — and the letter itself is a small masterpiece of precise, unsentimental analysis.

---

## The Voice: "The Writer"

The first thing you notice is the pronoun. Buffett writes "I" and "Charlie and I." Munger writes **"the writer"** — a formal, almost Victorian self-reference that appears throughout:

> "But, happily, the Berkshire Hathaway Insurance Group executives making original business-acquisition decisions are greatly admired and trusted by the writer..."
> "In the writer's judgment, the value of Wesco's advantage from its temporary, interest-free 'loan' was probably about $25 per Wesco share..."
> "Others may think differently, but the foregoing approach seems reasonable to the writer..."

This is not affectation. It's a lawyer's habit — Munger was a practicing attorney before he was an investor — and it creates a peculiar effect: the letter reads like a legal opinion written by someone who finds the whole enterprise slightly amusing. The distance is deliberate. He is analyzing Wesco, not selling it.

---

## The Results: Two Sets of Numbers, On Purpose

Munger presents earnings in a way Buffett rarely does — he splits them explicitly into "normal" and "irregularly occurring" items, and he does it in the first paragraph.

**"Normal" net operating income:** $38,262,000 ($5.38/share), up from $30,720,000 ($4.32/share).

**Consolidated net income:** $101,809,000 ($14.30/share), up from $30,619,000 ($4.30/share).

The gap: **$62,697,000 in realized net securities gains**, of which only **$93,000** came from actual sales. The balance — **$62,604,000** — came from the **Salomon/Travelers merger exchange**.

> "Accounting standards promulgated by the Financial Accounting Standards Board require that the fair (market) value of shares received in such an exchange be recorded as the new cost basis as of the date of the exchange, with the difference, after appropriate reserves for future income tax on the gain, recognized in the financial statements as a realized after-tax gain. For income tax purposes the exchange is recorded at the original cost of the securities exchanged; no gain is reported on the tax return, and no taxes are yet due."

This is the same distinction Buffett drew in the 1989 tax essay: accounting gain versus economic gain versus tax gain. Munger states it flatly and moves on — but the point is made. The $14.30 per share headline is mostly an accounting artifact. The $5.38 is the business.

---

## Wes-FIC and the "Fort Knox" Doctrine

The heart of the letter is the super-cat reinsurance business, and Munger's explanation of why Wes-FIC is structured the way it is.

> "Wes-FIC has a large net worth in relation to annual premiums being earned. And this is exactly the condition rationally required for any insurance company planning to be a 'stand alone' reinsurer covering super-catastrophe risks it can't safely pass on to others sure to remain solvent if a large super-catastrophe comes. Such a 'stand alone' reinsurer must be a kind of Fort Knox, prepared occasionally, without calling on any other reinsurers for help, to pay out in a single year many times more than premiums coming in, as it covers losses from some super catastrophe worse than Hurricane Andrew. In short, it needs a balance sheet a lot like Wes-FIC's."

**"A kind of Fort Knox."** That's the whole philosophy in one image. The point of holding enormous capital against tiny premiums is not to earn a return on the capital — it's to be *unconditionally able to pay* when the once-in-a-generation event arrives. That's a business model almost no public company can sustain, because it requires tolerating years of apparently idle capital.

The AAA rating from S&P in 1994 (the highest possible claims-paying-ability rating) is the external validation of that structure.

---

## The Berkshire/Wes-FIC Relationship: "The Punctilio of an Honor the Most Sensitive"

This is the letter's most delicate passage, and Munger handles it with characteristic precision.

The problem: Berkshire owns 100% of its Insurance Group but only 80% of Wesco/Wes-FIC. When Berkshire gets super-cat business it doesn't want to keep entirely, it *could* retrocede some to Wes-FIC — but that would mean giving value from a wholly-owned subsidiary to a partially-owned one, benefiting Wesco's minority shareholders at Berkshire's expense.

Munger's framing:

> "Berkshire Hathaway, owning 100% of its Insurance Group and only 80% of Wesco and Wes-FIC, does not, for some philanthropic reason, ordinarily retrocede to Wes-FIC any reinsurance business that Berkshire Hathaway considers desirable and that is available only in amounts below what Berkshire Hathaway wants for itself on the terms offered."

**"For some philanthropic reason"** — that's a joke, of course. The point is that Berkshire is *not* philanthropic with its own assets. Retrocessions happen only when:
1. Berkshire wants *less* business than is available (risk limitation)
2. Wes-FIC has adequate capacity
3. Wes-FIC pays a fair ceding commission

Then the Cardozo line:

> "Generally, Berkshire Hathaway, in dealing with partly owned subsidiaries, tries to lean over a little backward in an attempt to observe what Justice Cardozo called 'the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive,' but it cannot be expected to make large and plain giveaways of Berkshire Hathaway assets or business to a partially owned subsidiary like Wes-FIC."

**"The punctilio of an honor the most sensitive"** is from Cardozo's *Meinhard v. Salmon* (1928) — the most famous fiduciary duty case in American law, about a joint venture partner who secretly took a business opportunity for himself. Cardozo's full line: *"A trustee is held to something stricter than the morals of the marketplace. Not honesty alone, but the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive, is then the standard of behavior."*

Munger is quoting it deliberately. He's saying: Berkshire treats its partially-owned subsidiaries with the highest fiduciary standard the law recognizes — but that standard has limits, and it doesn't require giving away Berkshire assets.

**The "eating their own cooking" arrangement:**

> "It is impractical to have people in California make complex accept-or-reject decisions for Wes-FIC when retrocessions of reinsurance are offered by the Berkshire Hathaway Insurance Group. But, happily, the Berkshire Hathaway Insurance Group executives making original business-acquisition decisions are greatly admired and trusted by the writer and will be 'eating their own cooking.'"

So the mechanism: Berkshire executives make the decision, and Wesco's board has pre-approved automatic retrocessions on fixed terms — **3% ceding commission, Wes-FIC gets 20% or less of the risk, Berkshire retains at least 80%.**

This is governance by trusted-agent design. Munger's point is that the conflict of interest is real, so the structure is designed to make it benign: the same people making the original decisions are exposed to the outcome through Berkshire's 80% retention, and the terms are fixed in advance rather than negotiated case by case.

## The Honest Admission About "Bonanza"

> "And, again, we wish to repeat that we expect only acceptable long-term results. We see no possibility for bonanza."

That's Munger's characteristic deflation. He's telling Wesco shareholders, directly, not to expect spectacular returns from super-cat reinsurance. The business exists to provide a *modest* long-term return with occasional terrible years. That's it.

> "Moreover, Wesco shareholders should continue to realize that super-cat reinsurance is not for the faint of heart. A huge variation in annual results, with some very unpleasant future years for Wes-FIC, is inevitable."

---

## The Convertible Preferred Admission: "What Poor Prognosticators We Are"

This is the letter's best self-deprecating passage — and it's a direct callback to something Munger had written in earlier years.

> "In previous years we noted that 'few, if any, investors have ever prospered mightily from investing in convertible preferred stocks of leading corporations.' Our experience proves, yet again, what poor prognosticators we are."

**The results:**

| Investment | Cost | Yearend 1997 Value | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Travelers (via Salomon) | $80M net | ~$192.1M | +$112.1M |
| US Air | $12M | ~$19.2M | +$7.2M |
| Gillette (converted 1991) | $40M | $321.4M | +$281.4M |
| Champion (sold 1995) | $23M | — | +$6.9M pre-tax |

> "These figures when combined created $119.3 million more than actual cost. In addition, Wesco's investment in convertible preferred stock of The Gillette Company... is carried at a $321.4 million yearend market value... This is $281.4 million more than the investment cost."

So the "few, if any, investors have prospered mightily" prediction was wrong by roughly **$400 million**. Munger reports this with the same flat tone he uses for everything else, but the admission is significant: he was wrong, he was wrong in a specific and quantified way, and he says so without hedging.

---

## The Freddie Mac Footnote: The Accidental Masterpiece

Tucked into the "Tag Ends from Savings and Loan Days" section is one of the most extraordinary facts in the letter:

> "Of course, the main tag end from Wesco's savings and loan days is 28,800,000 shares of Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation ('Freddie Mac'), purchased by Mutual Savings for $72 million at a time when Freddie Mac shares could be lawfully owned only by a savings and loan association. This holding, with a market value of $1.2 billion at yearend 1997, now reposes in Wes-FIC."

**$72 million cost. $1.2 billion market value.** A 16.7x return.

And the reason it existed at all was a regulatory accident: Mutual Savings was an S&L, Freddie Mac shares could only be owned by S&Ls, and Wesco happened to own an S&L. That's the kind of "tag end" most companies write off as a legacy nuisance. Munger kept it, and it became the single largest asset in Wes-FIC.

(Note: this is the same Freddie Mac position that Berkshire itself held in the 1988–1992 letters. Munger's version, through Wesco, was even more concentrated.)

## The Intrinsic Value Calculation: The Most Explicit in Any Letter

This is where Munger is most unlike Buffett. Buffett rarely does explicit per-share intrinsic value arithmetic in public. Munger does it step by step.

**Step 1: Book value.** $248 per share at yearend 1997.

**Step 2: Liquidating value.** "The foregoing $248-per-share book value approximates liquidation value assuming that all Wesco's non-security assets would liquidate, after taxes, at book value. Probably, this assumption is too conservative. But our computation of liquidation value is unlikely to be too low by more than two or three dollars per Wesco share."

**Step 3: The interest-free "loan" from the government.**

> "So long as Wesco does not liquidate, and does not sell any appreciated assets, it has, in effect, an interest-free 'loan' from the government equal to its deferred income taxes on both the unrealized gains and gains deferred from the merger of Salomon into Travelers in 1997... This interest-free 'loan' from the government is at this moment working for Wesco shareholders and amounted to about $102 per Wesco share at yearend 1997."

**Step 4: But the loan isn't worth $102.**

> "However, some day, perhaps soon, major parts of the interest-free 'loan' must be paid as assets are sold. Therefore, Wesco's shareholders have no perpetual advantage creating value for them of $102 per Wesco share. Instead, the present value of Wesco's shareholders' advantage must logically be much lower than $102 per Wesco share. In the writer's judgment, the value of Wesco's advantage from its temporary, interest-free 'loan' was probably about $25 per Wesco share at yearend 1997."

**Step 5: The formula.**

> "This approximation is made by simply adding (1) the value of the advantage from the interest-free 'loan' per Wesco share and (2) liquidating value per Wesco share."

$248 + $25 = **$273 per share.**

**Step 6: Compare to market.**

> "This... figure for intrinsic per share value of Wesco stock should be compared with the $300 per share price at which Wesco stock was selling on December 31, 1997. This comparison indicates that Wesco stock was then selling about 10% above intrinsic value."

This is a complete, replicable, per-share intrinsic value calculation published in a public letter. It tells you exactly what Munger thinks the company is worth, exactly how he got there, and exactly how far the market price is from that figure. Almost no CEO does this.

---

## The Frank Admission: Wesco Is Not a Small Berkshire

> "Business and human quality in place at Wesco continues to be not nearly as good, all factors considered, as that in place at Berkshire Hathaway. Wesco is not an equally-good-but-smaller version of Berkshire Hathaway, better because its small size makes growth easier. Instead, each dollar of book value at Wesco continues plainly to provide much less intrinsic value than a similar dollar of book value at Berkshire Hathaway. Moreover, the quality disparity in book value's intrinsic merits has, in recent years, been widening in favor of Berkshire Hathaway."

This is remarkable. Munger is the Chairman of Wesco, writing to Wesco shareholders, telling them that their company is **inferior** to Berkshire — and that the gap is **widening**. He then declines to say which stock is the better buy at current prices:

> "All that said, we make no attempt to appraise relative attractiveness for investment of Wesco versus Berkshire Hathaway stock at present stock-market quotations."

That's the punctilio of honor again. He could have said "buy Berkshire." He didn't. He told the truth and left the decision to the shareholder.

## The Smaller Operating Businesses

**KBS (Kansas Bankers Surety):** Purchased July 1996 for ~$80 million. Contributed $6,044,000 in 1997 and $2,288,000 in 1996 (partial year). Chartered 1909 to underwrite deposit insurance for Kansas banks; now serves small and medium community banks across 25 mainly Midwestern states. Products: bank deposit guaranty bonds (insuring deposits above FDIC coverage), D&O indemnity, employment practices, annuity and mutual fund indemnity, insurance agents E&O. Run by Donald Towle, with 13 officers and employees.

**The Buffett lens:** This is exactly the kind of business Buffett describes in his acquisition criteria — small, niche, profitable, run by an able manager with a long tenure, in a business the parent understands. The "13 dedicated officers and employees" detail is Munger's way of saying: low overhead, high margin, no bureaucracy.

**Precision Steel:** $3,622,000 in normal net operating income, up 19% from $3,033,000. Driven by a 15% increase in pounds sold, with revenues up only 6.3% — meaning the business got more volume without pricing power. "Under the skilled leadership of David Hillstrom, Precision Steel's businesses in 1997 continued to provide an excellent return on resources employed."

(Note: Hillstrom is the same manager Buffett praised in the 1988 letter — "I think it is likely that his performance... would rank him number one among his peers.")

## How Munger's Mind Works (In This Letter)

**1. He separates accounting from economics explicitly and early.** The "normal" vs. "consolidated" split in the first paragraph is a structural choice — he wants you to see the business before he shows you the accounting artifacts.

**2. He designs governance around known conflicts.** The retrocession arrangement is a conflict of interest between Berkshire (100%) and Wesco (80%). Instead of pretending it doesn't exist, Munger builds a fixed-terms, pre-approved mechanism and explains exactly why it's fair. That's the "punctilio of an honor" in practice.

**3. He quantifies intrinsic value and publishes the arithmetic.** $248 + $25 = $273 vs. $300 market. The calculation is explicit, replicable, and includes his own judgment calls (the $25 figure) clearly labeled as such.

**4. He admits his predictions were wrong — with numbers.** "Few, if any, investors have ever prospered mightily from convertible preferreds" was wrong by roughly $400 million. He doesn't bury it; he headlines it.

**5. He is willing to say his own company is inferior.** "Wesco is not an equally-good-but-smaller version of Berkshire Hathaway." No chairman says this. Munger says it because it's true and because shareholders deserve to know.

**6. He treats the "Fort Knox" balance sheet as the product.** Wes-FIC's enormous capital relative to premiums isn't inefficiency — it's the thing being sold. Buyers of super-cat reinsurance are buying the certainty that the check will clear.

**7. He has a dry, legalistic humor.** "For some philanthropic reason." "The writer's judgment." "What poor prognosticators we are." The tone is that of a man who finds the world slightly absurd and has decided to describe it accurately anyway.

---

## The Key Quote

> "Such a 'stand alone' reinsurer must be a kind of Fort Knox, prepared occasionally, without calling on any other reinsurers for help, to pay out in a single year many times more than premiums coming in."

That's the whole philosophy of Berkshire's insurance operation — and of Berkshire itself — compressed into one image. The point of all that capital isn't to earn a high return. It's to be the entity that can *always* pay, and therefore the entity that gets the business nobody else can safely take.

But the sentence that best captures Munger's own character is this one:

> "Business and human quality in place at Wesco continues to be not nearly as good, all factors considered, as that in place at Berkshire Hathaway."

He is the Chairman of Wesco. He is writing to Wesco's shareholders. And he is telling them, plainly, that their company is not as good as the one he and Buffett run together. That is the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive, applied to himself.

## A Note on the Contrast with Buffett

Reading this alongside the Berkshire letters makes the partnership legible in a new way.

**Buffett narrates.** He tells you the story of the business, the managers, the mistakes, the jokes. His letters are warm, digressive, and confessional. He wants you to *like* the enterprise.

**Munger analyzes.** He gives you the numbers, the structure, the incentives, the legal standard, and the intrinsic value calculation. He wants you to *understand* the enterprise, and he is indifferent to whether you like it.

Buffett says: "Here's what happened and how it felt."
Munger says: "Here's what happened, here's what it's worth, here's why, and here's what could go wrong."

Both are necessary. Berkshire's letters are Buffett's; Wesco's letter is Munger's. And this one is a reminder that the analytical rigor underneath Berkshire's success was not incidental. It was Charlie's contribution, stated plainly, in his own hand.
