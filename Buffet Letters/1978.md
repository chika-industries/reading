# Analysis of the 1978 Berkshire Hathaway Letter

## A Transformative Year: The Merger with Diversified Retailing

The merger with Diversified Retailing Company at year-end 1978 fundamentally changed Berkshire's financial reporting and its ownership structure. Berkshire's ownership of Blue Chip Stamps increased to approximately **58%**, requiring full consolidation of Blue Chip's accounts (instead of the previous one‑line equity method). Through Blue Chip's 80% ownership of Wesco, Berkshire's effective ownership of Wesco is about 46%.

Buffett is characteristically blunt about the drawbacks of consolidated accounting:

> "Such a grouping of Balance Sheet and Earnings items – some wholly owned, some partly owned – tends to obscure economic reality more than illuminate it. In fact, it represents a form of presentation that we never prepare for internal use during the year and which is of no value to us in any management activities."

He promises to provide segmented information throughout the report so shareholders can see the businesses as he sees them.

## The Performance: 19.4% Return on Equity

Operating earnings (excluding capital gains) reached **19.4% of beginning shareholders' equity** – "within a fraction of our 1972 record." Over the last three years, Berkshire's per‑share net worth **virtually doubled** – a 25% annual compound – through a combination of operating earnings and capital gains.

But Buffett immediately warns: neither the 25% nor the 19.4% is sustainable.

> "The insurance cycle has turned downward in 1979, and it is almost certain that operating earnings measured by return on equity will fall this year. However, operating earnings measured in dollars are likely to increase on the much larger shareholders' equity now employed in the business."

**Equity holdings:** total cost $133.8 million, market value $220.9 million – a huge unrealized gain.

## The Sources of Earnings Table (A Buffett Innovation)

Two things stand out: the Buffalo Evening News is losing money (Buffett would later famously battle through years of losses to defeat a competitor), and See's Candies continues to be a gold mine.

## Textile Operations: The Economics of a Commodity Business

Earnings improved to $1.3 million after tax, but that's still a low return on the $17 million of capital employed. Buffett gives a textbook explanation of why commodity businesses are bad:

> "The textile industry illustrates in textbook style how producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real shortage. As long as excess productive capacity exists, prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed."

He notes that the plant and equipment are on the books for a tiny fraction of replacement cost – but that doesn't save them because capital turnover is slow. "Slow capital turnover, coupled with low profit margins on sales, inevitably produces inadequate returns on capital."

> "We hope we don't get into too many more businesses with such tough economic characteristics."

But again, he lists the four reasons for staying: (1) important employer in the community, (2) honest management, (3) cooperative labor, (4) modest cash returns.

## Insurance Underwriting: Phil Liesche's Extraordinary Year

The standout: Phil Liesche's segment produced about $90 million in earned premiums and an underwriting profit of approximately **$11 million** – "a truly extraordinary achievement."

Buffett gives credit to Jack Ringwalt, the founder, whose "operating philosophy remains etched upon the company."

**Worker's compensation:** Cypress Insurance (Milt Thornton) had outstanding results in its first year. Frank DeNardo was brought in to straighten out National Indemnity's California Worker's Comp business, which "had been a disaster." Volume is now only 25% of what it was eighteen months ago – a sign that they are cutting bad business.

**Reinsurance (George Young):** Still not satisfactory underwriting results, but generates large sums for investment. Buffett warns about self‑delusion in reserving: "It is very easy to fool yourself regarding underwriting results in reinsurance ... and we believe this situation prevails with many of our competitors."

**Homestate operation:** Disappointing in 1978, partially due to Midwestern storms. But Kansas Fire and Casualty, in its first full year, had "a truly remarkable start."

**The 1979 outlook:** The insurance cycle has turned. Rates are up only 3% while loss costs (auto repair, medical care) are up over 9%. "Margins will remain steady only if rates rise as fast as costs. This assuredly will not be the case in 1979."

**On expansion:** "Some of our expansion efforts – largely initiated by your Chairman – have been lackluster, others have been expensive failures." He concludes that "it is easier to buy a good insurance business than create one."

**SAFECO** gets special attention. Buffett calls it "probably the best run large property and casualty insurance company in the United States." He bought it at substantially under book value.

> "We paid less than 100 cents on the dollar for the best company in the business, when far more than 100 cents on the dollar is being paid for mediocre companies in corporate transactions."

**The passive ownership argument:**

> "Of course, with a minor interest we do not have the right to direct or even influence management policies of SAFECO. But why should we wish to do this? The record would indicate that they do a better job of managing their operations than we could do ourselves."

He notes that earnings attributable to SAFECO shares owned by Berkshire were $6.1 million in 1978, but only the dividends (about 18% of earnings) are reflected in operating earnings. The retained earnings are "just as real in terms of eventual benefit."

**Retained earnings of investees:**

> "Why should we feel differently about retention of earnings by companies in which we hold small equity interests, where the record indicates even better prospects for profitable employment of capital?"

This is a crucial insight. Buffett treats retained earnings of great companies as if Berkshire owned them directly – because eventually they will be reflected in market value.

**On market prices and pension funds:** Buffett notes with amusement that in 1971, pension funds put a record 122% of net funds into equities at full prices. In 1974, after the bottom fell out, they committed a record low 21%. In 1978, they put only 9% – another record low. "Pension managers, a group that logically should maintain the longest of investment perspectives" – buying low, selling high, but they do the opposite.

**Berkshire's policy:**
- Concentrate holdings, not diversify
- Buy worthwhile amounts when convinced of attractiveness
- Prefer prices to stay low because they are net buyers

## Banking: Gene Abegg's Last Years

Illinois National Bank earned about **2.1% of average assets** – about three times the level of major banks – with less risk. Since Berkshire purchased in 1969, consumer time deposits have quadrupled, net income tripled, trust income doubled, while costs were controlled.

> "Our experience has been that the manager of an already high-cost operation frequently is uncommonly resourceful in finding new ways to add to overhead, while the manager of a tightly-run operation usually continues to find additional methods to curtail costs, even when his costs are already well below those of his competitors. No one has demonstrated this latter ability better than Gene Abegg."

The bank must be divested by December 31, 1980 – most likely a spin‑off to Berkshire shareholders in the second half of 1980.

## Retailing: Ben Rosner at Associated Retail Stores

Associated Retail Stores (about 75 popular‑priced women's apparel stores) was launched in 1931 with one store, $3,200, and two partners: Ben Rosner and Leo Simon. Buffett acquired it through Diversified.

> "Ben's combination of merchandising, real estate and cost-containment skills has produced an outstanding record of profitability, with returns on capital necessarily employed in the business often in the 20% after‑tax area."

Ben is 75. Buffett notes that he, Gene Abegg (81), and Louie Vincenti (73) at Wesco "must appear to an outsider to be an overreaction on our part to an OEO bulletin on age discrimination." But:

> "It is a real pleasure to work with managers who enjoy coming to work each morning and, once there, instinctively and unerringly think like owners."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1978 Edition)

**1. He hates consolidated accounting that obscures economic reality.**
He says the GAAP presentation is "of no value to us in any management activities." He provides segmented data because he wants shareholders to understand the businesses the way he does.

**2. He distinguishes between operating earnings and total economic gains.**
Operating earnings are 19.4% of equity, but including capital gains, the three‑year compound is 25%. He reports both, but warns that neither is sustainable.

**3. He has a textbook understanding of commodity economics.**
The textile analysis is a miniature MBA lecture. Low differentiation + high fixed capital + excess capacity = low returns. He learned this lesson and applied it forever.

**4. He values great managers – even in mediocre businesses.**
Ben Rosner gets 20% after‑tax returns in a tough retailing environment. Buffett admires that enormously.

**5. He is humble about his own expansion failures.**
"Some of our expansion efforts – largely initiated by your Chairman – have been lackluster, others have been expensive failures." He names no names, but he takes the blame.

**6. He is a net buyer of stocks, so he wants prices low.**
> "We are not concerned with whether the market quickly revalues upward securities that we believe are selling at bargain prices. In fact, we prefer just the opposite."

**7. He sees retained earnings of great investees as his own.**
SAFECO's $6.1 million of earnings, of which Berkshire's dividend is only about $1.1 million – the rest is working for him, just not reported.

**8. He loves age and experience.**
The three 70+ managers – Abegg, Rosner, Vincenti – are not liabilities. They are competitive advantages.

## The Best Lines

> "We hope we don't get into too many more businesses with such tough economic characteristics."

A quiet prayer for avoiding commodity hell.

> "It is easier to buy a good insurance business than create one."

Learned through painful experience.

> "We paid less than 100 cents on the dollar for the best company in the business, when far more than 100 cents on the dollar is being paid for mediocre companies in corporate transactions."

The essence of his stock‑picking edge.

> "Why should we wish to direct or influence management of SAFECO? The record would indicate that they do a better job of managing their operations than we could do ourselves."

Humility and wisdom together.

## The Key Quote

> "We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price. When we are convinced as to attractiveness, we believe in buying worthwhile amounts."

Concentration, not diversification. Conviction, not hedging.

But the quote that best captures the letter's philosophy:

> "We are not at all unhappy when our wholly-owned businesses retain all of their earnings if they can utilize internally those funds at attractive rates. Why should we feel differently about retention of earnings by companies in which we hold small equity interests, where the record indicates even better prospects for profitable employment of capital?"

This is a profound point. The only difference between a wholly‑owned subsidiary and a minority stake in a public company is control. If the public company's management is outstanding and the business is wonderful, the retained earnings are just as valuable. Buffett treats them as his own.

## Transition Note

1978 was a year of structural change: the Diversified merger, full consolidation of Blue Chip, and a $220 million equity portfolio. The insurance cycle was peaking, and Buffett warned of a downturn ahead. The Buffalo Evening News was losing money (a story that would play out for years). But the foundation was set: See's Candies, SAFECO, Washington Post, GEICO, and a collection of brilliant old managers who thought like owners.

The next few years would test Buffett's patience. The insurance cycle would turn ugly. The Buffalo newspaper would bleed cash. But the compounding machine kept running.
