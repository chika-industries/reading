# Analysis of the 1980 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 27, 1981)

## The Big Theme: Non‑Controlled Ownership Earnings

This letter introduces a concept that would become central to Berkshire’s story: the huge and growing earnings of companies in which Berkshire owns less than 20% (and thus only dividends are reported). Buffett explains the three accounting categories:

- **Over 50% ownership** – full consolidation (Blue Chip Stamps, 60% owned).
- **20% to 50%** – proportional share of net income (Wesco, 48% owned).
- **Under 20%** – only dividends received.

Because Berkshire’s insurance subsidiaries concentrate their investments in the third category (wonderful businesses paying low dividends), **only a small portion of their current earnings appear in Berkshire’s operating earnings**. Yet those retained earnings are “as real as the dividends” – they build value.

> “The value to Berkshire Hathaway of retained earnings is not determined by whether we own 100%, 50%, 20% or 1% of the businesses in which they reside. Rather, the value of those retained earnings is determined by the use to which they are put and the subsequent level of earnings produced by that usage.”

The mind‑bending result: in 1980, the retained earnings of Berkshire’s non‑controlled holdings **exceeded total reported operating earnings of Berkshire Hathaway**. More than half of Berkshire’s economic earnings iceberg is below the waterline.

> “Our own analysis of earnings reality differs somewhat from generally accepted accounting principles … If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don’t record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree.”

## Operating Results: 17.8% Return on Beginning Equity

Operating earnings (excluding securities gains) rose to $41.9 million, but return on beginning equity (with securities at cost) fell from 18.6% to 17.8%. Buffett calls this the “most appropriate measure of single‑year managerial economic performance.”

Long‑term (since 1964), book value per share with equities at market has grown from $19.46 to **$400.80** – a 20.5% compounded annual rate. He adds a wry note: “You’ve done better: the value of the mineral content in the human body compounded at 22% annually during the past decade.”

## The Inflation Tax: Running Up a Down Escalator

This is one of Buffett’s most powerful explanations of how inflation destroys real returns even for profitable businesses. He defines the “investor’s misery index” as inflation plus the tax rate on dividends and capital gains.

> “In a world of 12% inflation a business earning 20% on equity (which very few manage consistently to do) and distributing it all to individuals in the 50% bracket is chewing up their real capital, not enhancing it. (Half of the 20% will go for income tax; the remaining 10% leaves the owners of the business with only 98% of the purchasing power they possessed at the start of the year – even though they have not spent a penny of their ‘earnings’).”

Worse, “explicit taxes are levied on nominal, not real, income.” So at high inflation, even a business earning a nominal 20% can produce a negative real after‑tax return.

> “At present inflation rates, we believe individual owners in medium or high tax brackets should expect no real long‑term return from the average American corporation.”

Berkshire has no corporate solution. “Inflation does not improve our return on equity.”

## GEICO: The Best of All Investment Worlds

Berkshire’s largest non‑controlled holding is 7.2 million shares of GEICO, about a 33% equity interest, acquired for $47 million. At the current dividend rate, reported earnings from GEICO are about $3 million annually – but Buffett estimates **Berkshire’s share of GEICO’s earning power is on the order of $20 million annually**. The undistributed portion ($17 million) is 40% of Berkshire’s total reported operating earnings.

He contrasts GEICO with a “true turnaround”:

> “Our conclusion is that, with few exceptions, when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact.”

GEICO is the exception because its fundamental low‑cost advantage was intact, only submerged by financial troubles. It was like American Express after the salad oil scandal – “extraordinary business franchises with a localized excisable cancer.”

> “What could be better than buying into a partnership with both [Jack Byrne and GEICO]?”

## The Insurance Industry’s Coming Bloodbath

The combined ratio rose to an estimated 103.5 in 1980, and Buffett predicts significantly worse in 1981 and 1982. But the real problem is the bond market. Many insurers carry long‑term bonds at amortized cost despite huge unrealized losses – in some cases, bonds at market would wipe out net worth.

> “Many insurers own long‑term bonds that, at amortized cost, amount to two to three times net worth. … Some of the largest and best known property‑casualty companies currently find themselves with nominal, or even negative, net worth when bond holdings are valued at market.”

This forces them into “asset maintenance underwriting” – accepting terrible business just to keep assets. The industry is trapped.

> “Right behind having financial problems yourself, the next worst plight is to have a large group of competitors with financial problems that they can defer by a ‘sell‑at‑any‑price’ policy.”

## Mistakes Acknowledged: Waumbec, Bonds, Homestate

Buffett admits the Waumbec acquisition was a “costly mistake.” Operations have been terminated. “Your Chairman made a costly mistake in not facing the realities of this situation sooner.”

On bonds: “We, too, lost important sums in bonds because your Chairman was talking when he should have been acting.”

The Homestate operation continues to have serious problems, particularly the Insurance Company of Iowa, which was abandoned.

## Banking Divestiture Completed

On December 31, 1980, Berkshire exchanged its bank shares for shares of Rockford Bancorp, allowing shareholders to maintain their proportional interest. Gene Abegg, founder and manager, died on July 2, 1980 at age 82. Buffett’s tribute to him is one of the most moving passages in any letter – full of admiration for his honesty, frugality, and fiduciary care.

> “Gene never forgot he was handling other people’s money. … Though this fiduciary attitude was always dominant, his superb managerial skills enabled the Bank to regularly achieve the top position nationally in profitability.”

## Financing: $60 Million of 12¾% Notes Due 2005

Berkshire borrowed not because of any specific need, but to have “plenty of financial firepower” when opportunities arise. This is classic Buffett: borrow when credit is available, not when you’re desperate.

> “Our acquisition preferences run toward businesses that generate cash, not those that consume it. … However attractive the earnings numbers, we remain leery of businesses that never seem able to convert such pretty numbers into no‑strings‑attached cash.”

## How Buffett’s Mind Works (1980 Edition)

**1. He sees through accounting to economic reality.**
The iceberg metaphor (most earnings hidden) and the tree‑in‑the‑forest analogy are brilliant. He doesn’t care what GAAP says – he cares what the businesses earn.

**2. He thinks in real, inflation‑adjusted terms.**
The 20% nominal return becomes zero real return after inflation and taxes. That changes everything. He is not celebrating nominal gains.

**3. He distinguishes between temporary problems and permanent advantages.**
GEICO had a temporary cancer, not a dead business. He is willing to buy a troubled franchise if the underlying economics are intact.

**4. He is humble about mistakes.**
Waumbec, bonds, Homestate – he names them, takes blame, and learns. “Turnarounds seldom turn” becomes a permanent rule.

**5. He values people over spreadsheets.**
The tribute to Gene Abegg is not about earnings – it’s about character, honesty, and fiduciary responsibility. Buffett invests in people he trusts.

**6. He prepares for the worst while hoping for the best.**
The $60 million debt is raised when credit is available, not when needed. He wants “plenty of financial firepower” for the inevitable opportunities.

## The Best Lines

> “If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don’t record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree.”

> “When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact.”

> “Right behind having financial problems yourself, the next worst plight is to have a large group of competitors with financial problems that they can defer by a ‘sell‑at‑any‑price’ policy.”

> “Your Chairman was talking when he should have been acting.”

> “Gene never forgot he was handling other people’s money.”

## The Key Quote

> “We would rather have earnings for which we did not get accounting credit put to good use in a 10%‑owned company by a management we did not personally hire, than have earnings for which we did get credit put into projects of more dubious potential by another management – even if we are that management.”

This is the mature Buffett. He doesn’t need control. He doesn’t need credit. He needs good capital allocation – wherever it happens. If a manager he admires can reinvest retained earnings at high returns, he is happy to own a small piece. That trust and humility are what made Berkshire a compounding machine.

---

## Transition Note

1980 was a year of transition. The bank was gone. Waumbec was shut down. The insurance industry was heading into a storm. But the GEICO stake was a jewel, the equity portfolio was strong, and Buffett’s philosophy was sharper than ever. The letters from this period are essential reading for anyone who wants to understand not just what Buffett bought, but how he thinks about inflation, accounting, and the difference between nominal and real returns.
