# Analysis of the 1981 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 26, 1982)

## The Performance: 15.2% Return on Equity

Operating earnings fell to $39.7 million, a **15.2% return on beginning equity** (securities at cost), down from 17.8% in 1980. The new shareholder charitable contribution program reduced earnings by about $900,000.

## Non-Controlled Ownership Earnings: The Iceberg Grows

Buffett reiterates his central theme: the retained earnings of non-controlled companies are as valuable as those of controlled subsidiaries, even though accounting rules ignore them.

> "If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree."

He notes that in aggregate, "our non-controlled business interests have more favorable underlying economic characteristics than our controlled businesses." Why? Because "small portions of exceptionally good businesses are usually available in the securities markets at reasonable prices. But such businesses are available for purchase in their entirety only rarely, and then almost always at high prices."

This is a crucial insight: the stock market offers better bargains for fractional ownership than the private market offers for whole companies.

## The Toad and Princess Theory of Acquisitions

This is one of Buffett's most famous and amusing passages. He identifies three motivations behind most high-premium takeovers:

1. **Animal spirits** – "Leaders, business or otherwise, seldom are deficient in animal spirits and often relish increased activity and challenge."

2. **Size obsession** – "Most organizations measure themselves, are measured by others, and compensate their managers far more by the yardstick of size than by any other yardstick."

3. **The "kiss the toad" delusion** – Managements believe their managerial prowess will transform a mediocre business into a prince.

> "Many managements apparently were overexposed in impressionable childhood years to the story in which the imprisoned handsome prince is released from a toad's body by a kiss from a beautiful princess. Consequently, they are certain their managerial kiss will do wonders for the profitability of Company T(arget)."

He adds wryly: "We've observed many kisses but very few miracles."

**The two categories of successful acquirers:**

1. **Category 1** – Companies that buy businesses with two characteristics:
   - Ability to raise prices easily without losing market share
   - Ability to handle large volume increases with minimal additional capital
   - (Examples: businesses with strong brands or franchises)

2. **Category 2** – Managerial superstars who can recognize princes disguised as toads and peel away the disguise. He names Ben Heineman (Northwest Industries), Henry Singleton (Teledyne), Erwin Zaban (National Service Industries), and especially **Tom Murphy at Capital Cities Communications** – "a real managerial 'twofer'."

But Buffett admits he doesn't qualify for Category 2. And while he understands Category 1, "our actual acquisition activity in that category has been sporadic and inadequate."

> "We neglected the Noah principle: predicting rain doesn't count, building arks does."

**The Berkshire acquisition philosophy:**

> "We will not normally pay a lot in any purchase for what we are supposed to bring to the party – for we find that we ordinarily don't bring a lot."

He admits they came close to a major purchase in 1981 but walked away because the price "would have left our owners worse off than before the purchase. The empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer."

## The Economic Value-Added Problem

This section is vintage Buffett – a sobering mathematical analysis of why most American businesses are now "bad" businesses for individual investors.

**The historical context:** Decades ago, long-term taxable bonds yielded 5% and tax-exempts 3%. A business earning 10% on equity was "good" – it earned more than passive money. Stocks sold at 150% of book value.

**The present reality:** Long-term tax-exempts yield 14%. American business earns about 14% on equity – before taxes. After taxes on dividends or capital gains, the individual investor gets far less than the tax-exempt bond yield.

> "Thus, with interest rates on passive investments at late 1981 levels, a typical American business is no longer worth one hundred cents on the dollar to owners who are individuals."

**The two scenarios:**

- If all earnings are paid out, a 14% pre-tax return becomes equivalent to a 7% tax-exempt bond for a 50% bracket investor – worth about 50 cents on the dollar.
- If all earnings are retained and grow at 14%, the stock price grows at 14% – but then the investor pays capital gains tax to realize it.

Either way, "the returns from passive capital outstrip the returns from active capital."

**The corporate tapeworm:** Inflation forces low-return businesses to retain all earnings just to stay alive – not because reinvestment is attractive, but because they need the capital to replace inflated working capital.

> "Inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism."

**Berkshire's position:** "We continue to clear the crossbar of after-tax passive return – but barely." A 21% compounded return is "not at all assured for the future." "It would be a bit humiliating to have our corporate value-added turn negative. But it can happen here."

## Insurance Industry Conditions: The Worst Year in History

Buffett forecasts that 1982 "will be the worst year in recent history for insurance underwriting." The data shows the problem:

| Year | Premium Written Change | Combined Ratio |
|------|------------------------|----------------|
| 1975 | 11.0% | 107.9 |
| 1976 | 21.9% | 102.4 |
| 1977 | 19.8% | 97.2 |
| 1978 | 12.8% | 97.5 |
| 1979 | 10.3% | 100.6 |
| 1980 | 6.0% | 103.1 |
| 1981 | 3.6% | 105.7 |

The collapse in premium growth (from 21.9% in 1976 to 3.6% in 1981) guarantees deteriorating results because rates are locked in for the life of the policy.

He also repeats the bond problem: insurers with huge unrealized bond losses are forced into "asset maintenance underwriting" – accepting bad business just to keep their asset base from shrinking.

> "Right behind having financial problems yourself, the next worst plight is to have a large group of competitors with financial problems that they can defer by a 'sell-at-any-price' policy."

Berkshire's position: low volume, maximum flexibility, financial strength that "could become an operational asset of immense value."

## Shareholder Designated Contributions

Berkshire introduced a program allowing shareholders to designate corporate charitable contributions – 95.6% of eligible shares responded. Buffett is delighted. He notes the "father-knows-best" school of corporate governance would be surprised that no one asked management to make the decisions.

But he also recounts a horror story: one large brokerage house transmitted his letter three weeks after receipt, too late for 60 shareholders to participate – but "it billed Berkshire for mailing services within six days of that belated and ineffectual action."

His advice: if you want to participate, register shares in your own name.

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1981 Edition)

**1. He thinks in after-tax, real (inflation-adjusted) terms.**
The entire "value-added" analysis is a devastating mathematical proof that most American businesses now destroy shareholder purchasing power. He is not celebrating nominal gains.

**2. He is brutally honest about his own limitations.**
"I don't qualify for Category 2." "We neglected the Noah principle." He knows what he can and cannot do.

**3. He mocks managerial hubris with the toad/princess analogy.**
This is Buffett's critique of empire-building CEOs who overpay for mediocre businesses, believing they can work miracles. He has seen "many kisses but very few miracles."

**4. He is willing to walk away from a deal.**
They almost bought a major business but "the empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer." Discipline matters more than size.

**5. He treats minority ownership as equal to control.**
The non-controlled ownership earnings are "fully as important" as reported earnings. He doesn't need to run the business to benefit from it.

**6. He values shareholder engagement.**
The charitable contributions program got an extraordinary response. He is proud of that.

## The Key Quote

> "Regardless of the impact upon immediately reportable earnings, we would rather buy 10% of Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X per share. Most corporate managers prefer just the reverse."

This is the essence of Buffett's philosophy: economic substance over accounting appearance, value over control, partnership over empire. He doesn't need to own it all to benefit from it. He just needs the price to be right.
