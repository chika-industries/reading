# Analysis of the 1982 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (March 3, 1983)

## The Performance: A Sharp Decline to 9.8%

Operating earnings fell to $31.5 million – a **9.8% return on beginning equity capital** (securities at cost), down from 15.2% in 1981 and far below the 1978 peak of 19.4%.

Buffett gives three reasons:
1. Significant deterioration in insurance underwriting results
2. Considerable expansion of equity capital without corresponding growth in directly operated businesses
3. A continually enlarging commitment to partially‑owned, non‑operated businesses – where accounting rules exclude most of Berkshire's share of earnings

And then he does something unusual: **he abandons his own yardstick.**

> "It was only a few years ago that we told you that the operating earnings/equity capital percentage... was the most important yardstick of single-year managerial performance. While we still believe this to be the case with the vast majority of companies, we believe its utility in our own case has greatly diminished."

He acknowledges how this must look:

> "You should be suspicious of such an assertion. Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding favorable readings. But when results deteriorate, most managers favor disposition of the yardstick rather than disposition of the manager."

He then offers one of his most famous metaphors for accounting manipulation:

> "To managers faced with such deterioration, a more flexible measurement system often suggests itself: just shoot the arrow of business performance into a blank canvas and then carefully draw the bullseye around the implanted arrow."

Berkshire, he insists, prefers "pre‑set, long‑lived and small bullseyes." But in this case, the abandonment is warranted – because the real earnings are invisible to GAAP.

## Non‑Reported Ownership Earnings: The GEICO Illustration

Buffett uses GEICO to show how badly accounting can misrepresent reality.

Berkshire owns about **35% of GEICO**, but because voting rights were assigned to an independent party (per insurance department requirements), GEICO is treated as a less‑than‑20% holding for accounting purposes.

**The perverse result:**
- Dividends received from GEICO in 1982: **$3.5 million after tax** – the only item in Berkshire's reported earnings
- Berkshire's share of GEICO's undistributed operating earnings: **$23 million** – totally excluded
- If GEICO had earned less but paid more dividends, Berkshire's reported earnings would have been *higher*
- If GEICO had earned an additional $100 million and retained it all, reported earnings would have been *unchanged*

> "Clearly 'accounting' earnings can seriously misrepresent economic reality."

**The four major non‑controlled holdings** (GEICO, General Foods, Washington Post, R.J. Reynolds):
- Berkshire's share of undistributed 1982 operating earnings: **well over $40 million**
- Dividends received from these companies: only **$14 million**
- So the unreported earnings are nearly **3x the reported dividends** – and greater than Berkshire's *total* reported earnings

> "This number – not reflected at all in our earnings – is greater than our total reported earnings."

He is careful not to over-claim:

> "We attach real significance to the general magnitude of these numbers, but we don't believe they should be carried to ten decimal places."

Because realization of these retained earnings through market appreciation is subject to "very substantial, but indeterminate, taxation" and is "extraordinarily uneven among companies and irregular and unpredictable in timing."

## The Manic‑Depressive Lemmings

One of Buffett's most memorable lines describes the stock market:

> "This investor may select from almost the entire array of major American corporations... And fractional-interest purchases can be made in an auction market where prices are set by participants with behavior patterns that sometimes resemble those of an army of manic-depressive lemmings."

He also introduces **St. Offset** – "Arthur Okun's patron saint for economists" – acknowledging that some retained earnings have produced zero or negative market value, while others have produced two or more dollars of value per dollar retained. So far, "our corporate over-achievers have more than offset the laggards."

## The Pascal Quote and the Blown Deal

Buffett admits he "left the room once too often last year" and almost starred in the "Acquisition Follies of 1982."

> "In retrospect, our major accomplishment of the year was that a very large purchase to which we had firmly committed was unable to be completed for reasons totally beyond our control."

He wishes he could illustrate it with "two blank pages depicting this blown deal" as the centerfold.

Then the Pascal quote:

> "It has struck me that all men's misfortunes spring from the single cause that they are unable to stay quietly in one room."

And his own gloss on others' 1982 acquisitions:

> "For in many of these acquisitions, managerial intellect wilted in competition with managerial adrenaline. The thrill of the chase blinded the pursuers to the consequences of the catch."

## Long‑Term Performance: 22.0% Compounded

Net worth gain in 1982: **$208 million** on a beginning base of $519 million – a **40% gain** (boosted by market appreciation of equities).

Over 18 years of present management: book value has grown from $19.46 to **$737.43 per share** – **22.0% compounded annually**.

> "You can be certain that this percentage will diminish in the future. Geometric progressions eventually forge their own anchors."

**GEICO again:** $79 million of the $208 million gain came from GEICO's market price increase. Buffett notes the market gain has exceeded the gain in intrinsic business value – but he expected this convergence, and looks forward to more gains in underlying business value.

> "Although not found in the catechism of the better business schools, 'Let Jack Do It' works fine as a corporate creed for us."

## Sources of Earnings: Insurance Underwriting Collapses

The table shows the damage clearly:
- Insurance underwriting: **($21.6 million)** pre‑tax – a massive swing from +$1.5 million in 1981
- Net investment income: $41.6 million (up from $38.8 million)
- Textiles: ($1.5 million) – still losing money
- See's Candies: $14.2 million (Berkshire share, up from $12.5 million)
- Buffalo Evening News: ($0.7 million) – still losing, but improving

Berkshire's share of operating earnings: **$27.7 million** pre‑tax, down from $47.2 million in 1981.

## The Buffalo News Triumph

A rare bright spot: the Buffalo Evening News now has Sunday circulation of **367,000** – up 35% in six years – while the competing Courier‑Express had 272,000. Buffett notes:

> "We know of no city in the United States with a long history of seven-day newspaper publication in which the percentage of households purchasing the Sunday newspaper has grown at anything like this rate."

He credits Henry Urban, Stan Lipsey, Murray Light, Clyde Pinson, Dave Perona and Dick Feather.

## Insurance Industry Conditions: The End of the Old Order

This is one of the most important sections in any Berkshire letter. Buffett argues that the insurance industry's historical profitability was an artifact of a **legal quasi‑administered pricing system** – and that system is gone.

**The old system:**
- For much of the century, rates were set through negotiations between industry "bureaus" and state regulators
- "Dignified haggling occurred, but it was between company and regulator rather than between company and customer"
- "When the dust settled, Giant A charged the same price as Giant B – and both companies and agents were prohibited by law from cutting such filed rates"
- Result: companies could "legally price their way to profitability even in the face of substantial over-capacity"

**The new reality:**
- New capacity exists outside the old structure
- It uses price as "a prime competitive weapon"
- "Customers have learned that insurance is no longer a one-price business. They won't forget"

**The commodity industry analysis:**

Buffett gives a textbook explanation of why industries with over-capacity and commodity products are terrible:

> "Businesses in industries with both substantial over-capacity and a 'commodity' product... are prime candidates for profit troubles."

Insurance fits this perfectly:
- Capacity can be "instantly created by capital plus an underwriter's willingness to sign his name"
- The product is largely undifferentiated
- "Many insureds, including the managers of large businesses, do not even know the names of their insurers"

> "Insurance, therefore, would seem to be a textbook case of an industry usually faced with the deadly combination of excess capacity and a 'commodity' product."

**Why was it profitable historically?** Because of the regulatory system described above.

**Why won't it be profitable in the future?**

> "Instead, major capacity withdrawals require a shock factor such as a natural or financial 'megadisaster'. One might occur tomorrow – or many years from now. The insurance business – even taking investment income into account – will not be particularly profitable in the meantime."

**The opportunity:** When supply ultimately contracts, "large amounts of business will be available for the few with large capital capacity, a willingness to commit it, and an in-place distribution system." Berkshire expects "great opportunities" at that time.

**Berkshire's 1982 performance:** Underwriting deteriorated "far more than did the industry's." From above-average to "modestly below average." The biggest swing was in National Indemnity's traditional coverages.

**The stars:** Milt Thornton (Cypress) and Floyd Taylor (Kansas Fire & Casualty) "simply are incapable of being average." Mike Goldberg took over parent company responsibility for insurance operations, and planning, recruitment, and monitoring improved.

**GEICO:** Jack Byrne and Bill Snyder are "achieving the most elusive of human goals – keeping things simple and remembering what you set out to do." Lou Simpson is "the best investment manager in the property-casualty business."

> "GEICO is a magnificent illustration of the high-profit exception... – a company with a wide and sustainable cost advantage."

## Issuance of Equity: The Golden Rule

This is one of the most important sections in Berkshire's history. Buffett lays out his rule for issuing shares:

> "We will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business value as we give."

He then explains why this seemingly obvious rule is violated constantly:

**The asymmetry:** When a company sells itself entirely, it gets full business value. But when it issues shares for an acquisition, it gets only market value – which may be far below intrinsic value.

> "The acquirer who nevertheless barges ahead ends up using an undervalued (market value) currency to pay for a fully valued (negotiated value) property. In effect, the acquirer must give up $2 of value to receive $1 of value."

**The rationalizations** (which he demolishes one by one):

(a) "The company we're buying is going to be worth a lot more in the future." – Presumably so is the interest in the old business being traded away.

(b) "We have to grow." – "Who, it might be asked, is the 'we'? For present shareholders, the reality is that all existing businesses shrink when shares are issued." He gives the farm analogy: merge your 120-acre farm with a neighbor's 60 acres into an equal partnership, and your managerial domain grows to 180 acres but your family's ownership shrinks by 25%.

(c) "Our stock is undervalued and we've minimized its use in this deal." – "A man is not charmed if a spaniel defaces his lawn, just because it's a spaniel and not a St. Bernard."

**The three solutions:**
1. A true business-value-for-business-value merger (rare)
2. Acquirer's stock sells at or above intrinsic value (rare in recent years)
3. Go ahead, then repurchase an equal number of shares afterward ("damage-repair")

**The "double whammy":** First, the loss of intrinsic value from the merger. Second, the downward revision in market valuation given to a management with a record of wealth destruction.

> "Once management shows itself insensitive to the interests of owners, shareholders will suffer a long time from the price/value ratio afforded their stock."

**The restaurant analogy:**

> "Those assurances are treated by the market much as one-bug-in-the-salad explanations are treated at restaurants."

## The Acquisition Criteria (The Famous List)

This is where Buffett first publishes his acquisition criteria:

1. Large purchases (at least $5 million of after-tax earnings)
2. Demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest, nor are "turn-around" situations)
3. Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt
4. Management in place (we can't supply it)
5. Simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)
6. An offering price (we don't want to waste our time talking when price is unknown)

He adds:
> "We will not engage in unfriendly transactions. We can promise complete confidentiality and a very fast answer as to possible interest – customarily within five minutes."

**Charitable contributions:** 95.8% of eligible shares participated, even though only $1 per share was available (down from $2).

**Office expansion:** "In a characteristically rash move, we have expanded World Headquarters by 252 square feet (17%)."

**The five people who work with Buffett:** Joan Atherton, Mike Goldberg, Gladys Kaiser, Verne McKenzie, Bill Scott – "outproduce corporate groups many times their number."

**Charlie Munger:** "Charlie and I are interchangeable in business decisions. Distance impedes us not at all: we've always found a telephone call to be more productive than a half-day committee meeting."

**Two retirements:** Phil Liesche (National Indemnity) and Ben Rosner (Associated Retail Stores).

> "Both Ben and Phil ran their businesses for Berkshire with every bit of the care and drive that they would have exhibited had they personally owned 100% of these businesses. No rules were necessary to enforce or even encourage this attitude; it was embedded in the character of these men long before we came on the scene. Their good character became our good fortune."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1982 Edition)

**1. He is willing to abandon his own yardstick – and tells you why.**
The operating earnings/equity ratio no longer captures Berkshire's reality because of the growing importance of non-controlled earnings. He doesn't hide the change; he explains it.

**2. He sees accounting as a starting point, not an ending point.**
The GEICO example is devastating. Accounting earnings can go up while business results go down. He cares about economic reality.

**3. He thinks in terms of opportunity cost and value exchange.**
The share issuance section is a masterclass. Every acquisition funded with undervalued stock destroys value. He has a simple rule: get as much as you give.

**4. He understands industry structure.**
The insurance analysis is profound. The old regulatory system created artificial profitability. That system is gone. The industry is now a commodity business with over-capacity – and will be unprofitable until capacity contracts.

**5. He is patient and prepared.**
The "megadisaster" will come. When it does, Berkshire will have capital, distribution, and willingness to write business at proper prices.

**6. He values character and loyalty.**
The tributes to Phil Liesche and Ben Rosner are moving. These men worked for Berkshire as if they owned it. That's the highest praise Buffett can give.

**7. He has a sense of humor about his own mistakes.**
The "Acquisition Follies of 1982" and the two blank pages for the blown deal are self-deprecating and funny.

## The Key Quote

> "We will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business value as we give."

This is the shareholder's golden rule. It seems obvious. It is violated constantly. And it explains why Berkshire's shareholders have been so well served.

But the most profound insight of the letter may be this:

> "Managers and investors alike must understand that accounting numbers are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation."

That is the thread that runs through everything – the non-controlled earnings, the GEICO example, the share issuance analysis. Accounting is a language, not reality. Buffett reads the language and translates it into economic truth.

## Transition Note

1982 was a turning point. The insurance industry's old pricing system was gone. Buffett had abandoned his own yardstick because it no longer captured reality. The acquisition criteria were published. And the share issuance philosophy was stated in its final form.

The seeds of future greatness were also visible: GEICO at $309 million market value (cost: $47 million), Washington Post at $103 million (cost: $10.6 million), and the Buffalo News finally gaining traction.

Buffett was building not just a portfolio, but a philosophy – and he was teaching it to his shareholders one letter at a time.
