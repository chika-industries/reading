# Analysis of the 1987 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 29, 1988)

## The Performance: 19.5% Gain, but Business Value Leads Book Value

Net worth gain: **$464 million**, or **19.5%**. Over 23 years, book value per share grew from $19.46 to **$2,477.47** – **23.1% compounded annually**.

But Buffett immediately shifts focus:

> "What counts, of course, is the rate of gain in per-share business value, not book value. In many cases, a corporation's book value and business value are almost totally unrelated."

He gives examples: LTV and Baldwin-United had large book values just before bankruptcy. Belridge Oil sold for $3.6 billion with book value of only $177 million. At Berkshire, business value has consistently exceeded book value, and the premium widened in 1987.

Why? "We own some remarkable businesses and they are run by even more remarkable managers."

## The Sainted Seven: 57% Return on Equity

Buffett highlights seven non-financial units: Buffalo News, Fechheimer, Kirby, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing Group, See's Candies, and World Book.

In 1987, these seven had combined operating earnings before interest and taxes of **$180 million**. Net interest expense was only **$2 million**. So pre-tax earnings on equity capital employed were **$178 million** – on equity of only **$175 million** (historical-cost basis).

If operated as a single company, after-tax earnings would have been about **$100 million** – a return of about **57% on equity capital**.

For context, Fortune reported that among the 1,000 largest industrial and service companies, only six had averaged over 30% ROE during the previous decade. The best was Commerce Clearing House at 40.2%.

Buffett notes Berkshire paid a premium of about **$222 million** above historical accounting values for these businesses, so Berkshire’s returns are lower. But the managers should be judged on the returns they achieve on underlying assets.

Three inferences:
1. Current business value far exceeds historical book value and balance-sheet carrying value.
2. These businesses require little capital, so they can grow while sending most earnings to Berkshire for redeployment.
3. They are run by truly extraordinary managers.

> "The Blumkins, the Heldmans, Chuck Huggins, Stan Lipsey, and Ralph Schey all meld unusual talent, energy and character to achieve exceptional financial results."

Buffett quotes Jack Benny: "I don't deserve this, but then, I have arthritis and I don't deserve that either."

## Managerial Philosophy: Applause, Not Committees

Buffett and Munger have little to do with operations. "We have no corporate meetings, no corporate budgets, and no performance reviews."

> "After all, what can we tell the Blumkins about home furnishings, or the Heldmans about uniforms?"

Their main contribution is applause – but not indiscriminate applause. "It is informed applause based upon the two long careers we have spent intensively observing business performance and managerial behavior."

## Sources of Earnings: The Operating Businesses

The table shows all major units. Key updates:

**Nebraska Furniture Mart:** Sales $142.6 million, up 8%. Mrs. B is 94, works seven days a week. Buffett persuaded the Board to scrap mandatory retirement-at-100. "With every passing year, this policy has seemed sillier to me."

Mrs. B's maxim: "Sell cheap and tell the truth." Customers saved about $30 million in 1987. An anonymous competitor wrote complaining NFM was leaving 10-20% on the table. Buffett's response: NFM will continue to grow by following Mrs. B's maxim.

**Buffalo News:** National leader in penetration and news-hole percentage. News hole ~50% vs. typical 40%. Cutting it would save $4 million annually in newsprint. "That interests us not at all." They redo the obituary page seven times a day. Stan Lipsey has produced excellent profit growth, but margins will shrink in 1988 due to newsprint costs.

**Fechheimer:** Another family business – three generations of Heldmans. Profits increased substantially in 1987. "There's nothing magic about the Uniform business; the only magic is in the Heldmans."

**See's:** Volume hit new high at slightly under 25 million pounds. Same-store sales virtually unchanged for second year – improvement after six years of decline. 85% of profit earned in December. Chuck Huggins remains 100% customer-oriented. When See's eliminated 14 candy items and customers protested, Chuck reintroduced them and turned the miscue into an opportunity. "Fortunately, when I make poor decisions, good things often happen as a result."

**World Book / Kirby / Scott Fetzer:** Ralph Schey manages 19 businesses. Pre-tax earnings rose 10% while average capital employed declined. World Book introduced its most revised edition since 1962; U.S. unit sales increased for fifth consecutive year. Kirby had fourth straight year of unit volume gains.

## Insurance: Commodity Economics and the Coming Downturn

Industry combined ratio improved to 104.7 in 1987, but premium growth was slowing: 12.9%, 11.1%, 5.7%, 5.6% by quarter. "Clearly, the party is over."

Buffett explains the industry’s dismal long-term economics: hundreds of competitors, ease of entry, undifferentiated product. Only low-cost operators or protected niches can sustain high profitability. When shortages exist, even commodity businesses flourish – but managers can’t wait to expand capacity and plug the hole.

> "Disraeli's observation: 'What we learn from history is that we do not learn from history.'"

Berkshire differentiates in two ways:
1. **Financial strength** – exceeds all others. Matters when buyers focus on whether a $10 million claim can be paid years later.
2. **Indifference to volume** – willing to write five times as much or one-fifth as much. No other major insurer acts with equal restraint.

Berkshire’s combined ratio was 105, but float grew, so profits improved. They expect profit improvement in 1988-89 even as combined ratio rises.

**Loss reserving:** Buffett criticizes auditors for certifying casualty insurer earnings they cannot accurately assess. He suggests the standard opinion letter should read: "We can express no opinion about the accuracy of these figures." He notes deliberate fraudsters exploit long-tail insurance. "Where 'earnings' can be created by the stroke of a pen, the dishonest will gather."

**Taxes:** The 1986 Tax Reform Act hurt insurance long-term economics. The 1987 Bill further reduced intercorporate dividends-received credit.

## Marketable Securities – Permanent Holdings

Berkshire’s three permanent common stock holdings:

| Company | Cost (000s) | Market (000s) |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| Capital Cities/ABC | $517,500 | $1,035,000 |
| GEICO | $45,713 | $756,925 |
| Washington Post | $9,731 | $323,092 |

Market value over **$2 billion**. Yet they produced only **$11 million in reported after-tax earnings** for Berkshire in 1987. Accounting rules count only dividends, not Berkshire’s share of earnings (over $100 million).

> "Accounting rules let us reflect in our net worth the up-to-date underlying values of the businesses we partially own, but do not let us reflect their underlying earnings in our income account."

For controlled companies, the opposite is true: full earnings in income, but no upward revaluation on balance sheet.

Buffett says they ignore GAAP and focus on future earning power.

**Controlled vs. marketable:**
- Controlled companies allow Berkshire to allocate capital. Most CEOs lack capital-allocation skills.
- Controlled companies offer tax advantages (80%+ ownership).
- Marketable securities sometimes offer ridiculous prices – e.g., Washington Post bought at $5.63 per share, 1987 after-tax operating earnings $10.30; GEICO bought at average $6.67, 1987 earnings $9.01.

## Mr. Market: The Best Explanation of Market Fluctuations

Buffett quotes Ben Graham’s Mr. Market allegory at length. Mr. Market is your partner who daily offers to buy or sell. He has incurable emotional problems – sometimes euphoric, sometimes depressed. He doesn’t mind being ignored. The more manic-depressive he is, the better for you.

> "But, like Cinderella at the ball, you must heed one warning or everything will turn into pumpkins and mice: Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful."

> "If you aren't certain that you understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don't belong in the game. As they say in poker, 'If you've been in the game 30 minutes and you don't know who the patsy is, you're the patsy.'"

Buffett criticizes portfolio insurance: selling as prices decline. "After buying a farm, would a rational owner next order his real estate agent to start selling off pieces of it whenever a neighboring property was sold at a lower price?" He calls it Alice-in-Wonderland. Volatility from irrational money managers offers opportunities to true investors.

At yearend 1987, Berkshire had no major common stock investments (over $50 million) other than permanent or arbitrage holdings. "Mr. Market will offer us opportunities – you can be sure of that."

## Other Investments: Bonds, Arbitrage, Texaco, Salomon

- **Medium-term tax-exempt bonds:** ~$900 million parking place. Limited virtues.
- **Long-term bonds:** Aversion due to inflation risk and trade deficit. "Bonds are no better than the currency in which they are denominated."
- **WPPSS bonds:** Added in 1987. Amortized cost $240 million, market $316 million, annual tax-exempt income $34 million.
- **Arbitrage:** Limited scale, only large announced deals. Average pre-tax returns at least 25% historically. Only major position: Allegis.
- **Texaco bonds:** Purchased after bankruptcy. Carried at $104 million, market $119 million.
- **Salomon preferred:** $700 million purchase of 9% convertible preferred. John Gutfreund praised for integrity. Valued at 98% of par at yearend.

## Financing and Debt Policy

Berkshire sold $250 million of debentures due 2018, overall cost slightly over 10%. Despite pessimistic inflation views, debt taste is limited.

> "We do not wish it to be only likely that we can meet our obligations; we wish that to be certain."

They follow policies that allow acceptable long-term results under extraordinarily adverse conditions. "Good business or investment decisions will eventually produce quite satisfactory economic results, with no aid from leverage."

They prefer to finance in anticipation of need, not reaction. "If you want to shoot rare, fast-moving elephants, you should always carry a loaded gun." The $250 million raised at 10% is currently earning 6.5%, costing about $160,000 per week. That negative spread is unimportant.

## Miscellaneous: Harry Bottle, Charitable Contributions, Annual Meeting

Buffett repeats acquisition criteria: $10 million after-tax earnings, consistent earning power, good ROE with little debt, management in place, simple businesses, offering price.

A "deja vu" story: In 1962, Harry Bottle rescued Dempster Mill. In 1987, Charlie called Harry, now 68, to rescue K&W Products. K&W profits set a record, up more than 300%, while capital employed decreased 20%. "If we run into another managerial problem ten or twenty years down the road, you know whose phone will ring."

Charitable contributions: 97.2% participation, $4.9 million to 2,050 charities. Buffett criticizes corporate matching of director contributions: "When A takes money from B to give to C and A is a legislator, the process is called taxation. But when A is an officer or director of a corporation, it is called philanthropy."

Annual meeting May 23, 1988. Buses to Furniture Mart. Mrs. B says shareholders must beat last year's sales figures.

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1987 Edition)

**1. He values business value over book value.**
The Sainted Seven earn 57% on equity. The premium of business value over book value widened again.

**2. He admires and trusts great managers – and gives them freedom.**
No meetings, budgets, or performance reviews. "Our major contribution is applause."

**3. He thinks in terms of commodity economics.**
Insurance is a commodity business. Only low-cost operators or protected niches thrive. Shortages create temporary profits, but managers rush to expand capacity and kill the party.

**4. He is a fierce critic of Wall Street practices.**
Portfolio insurance, "cash flow" illusions, auditors certifying unreserveable numbers, greenmail – he sees through it all.

**5. He uses Mr. Market as a mental model.**
Market fluctuations are opportunities, not instructions. "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you."

**6. He is conservative with debt.**
He wants certainty of meeting obligations, not likelihood. He finances in anticipation of need.

**7. He values character and loyalty.**
Tom Murphy, Dan Burke, Bill Snyder, Lou Simpson, Kay Graham, John Gutfreund – he invests with people he likes and admires.

**8. He has a sense of humor.**
Jack Benny, Agatha Christie, Mrs. B, the "patsy" poker line, the "loaded gun" elephant.

## The Key Quote

> "In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine." (Ben Graham, quoted by Buffett)

But the most important passage for investors is:

> "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful."

That is the essence of Buffett’s approach to markets. Price fluctuations are opportunities to buy or sell – not signals about value. The investor who understands this has a permanent advantage over those who let Mr. Market guide them.

## Transition Note

1987 was the year of the October crash, but Buffett barely mentions it except as context. He had no major new common stock purchases because prices rebounded too quickly. He remained patient, holding permanent stakes in Cap Cities, GEICO, and Washington Post, and parking money in medium-term tax-exempts.

The letter is a masterclass in business analysis, market psychology, and managerial admiration. The Sainted Seven, Mr. Market, the insurance commodity cycle, the Salomon investment, the debt policy – all of it reflects a mature Buffett at the height of his powers. He is not just picking stocks; he is teaching a philosophy.
