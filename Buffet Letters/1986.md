# Analysis of the 1986 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (February 27, 1987)

## The Performance: 26.1% Gain, But the Real Story Is Capital Allocation

Net worth gain: **$492.5 million** or **26.1%**. Over 22 years, book value per share grew from $19.46 to **$2,073.06** – **23.3% compounded annually**. Corporate net worth increased 10,600% while shares outstanding increased less than 1%.

Buffett notes that book value has been a "reasonable if somewhat conservative proxy" for intrinsic business value for over a decade. In 1986, business value probably grew even faster than book value, thanks to the managers of the operating businesses.

But then the bad news:

> "The bad news is that my performance did not match that of our managers. While they were doing a superb job in running our businesses, I was unable to skillfully deploy much of the capital they generated."

## The Two Jobs: Managers and Capital Allocation

Charlie and I have only two jobs:
1. **Attract and keep outstanding managers.** This hasn't been difficult. "They were managerial stars long before they knew us, and our main contribution has been to not get in their way."
2. **Allocate capital.** This is the hard part at Berkshire because: (a) they earn more than average, (b) they retain all earnings, and (c) the businesses require little incremental capital.

> "If our retained earnings – and those of our major investees, GEICO and Capital Cities/ABC – are employed in an unproductive manner, the economics of Berkshire will deteriorate very quickly."

Capital allocation in 1986 was "tough work." Only one acquisition (Fechheimer, small). No new marketable equities. So they paid off debt and stockpiled funds. "Neither is a fate worse than death, but they do not inspire us to do handsprings either."

## Managerial Style: Hire Giants, Work with People You Like

Buffett quotes David Ogilvy: "If each of us hires people who are bigger than we are, we shall become a company of giants."

He says they can work with double the number of managers if they have the rare qualities of the present ones. "Working with people who cause your stomach to churn seems much like marrying for money – probably a bad idea under any circumstances, but absolute madness if you are already rich."

The Catholic tailor joke: A tailor saved for years to visit the Vatican. Upon returning, his parish asked what the Pope was like. "He's a forty-four, medium."

## Sources of Earnings: The Operating Businesses Shine

The table now includes segments from Scott Fetzer and Fechheimer. Operating earnings improved substantially. Key units:

**Buffalo News:** Superb job by Stan Lipsey. Man-hours fell, costs controlled, margins improved. News hole maintained at 50% – "we end up delivering our readers no less than 50% more news" than typical papers. Highest weekday penetration among top 50 papers. Sunday penetration 83%, up from Courier-Express's 63%. "Our operating margins almost certainly have peaked" due to newsprint price increases.

**Nebraska Furniture Mart:** Sales up 10.2% to $132 million. Mrs. B at 93, seven days a week. "Competing with her represents a triumph of courage over judgment." Buffett jokes: "At 93, Omaha based Board Chairmen have yet to reach their peak. Please file this fact away to consult before you mark your ballot at the 2024 annual meeting of Berkshire."

**See's:** Total pounds sold rose 2%. Same-store sales virtually unchanged, ending a six-year decline. 12,000 tons annually. Chuck Huggins praised for customer-oriented spirit.

**World Book:** Unit volume up 7% over 1985, 45% over 1982. Dominates U.S. direct-sales encyclopedia market. Priced under 5 cents per page. "Selling World Book is a calling. Over one-half of our active salespeople are teachers or former teachers."

**Kirby:** Fourth straight year of unit volume gains. Up 4% from 1985, 33% from 1982. "If you want the best, you buy a Kirby."

## Fechheimer Bros.: The Ad Pays Off

Bob Heldman, a longtime shareholder, wrote to Buffett suggesting a meeting. Fechheimer is a uniform manufacturing and distribution business founded in 1842. Family-run, excellent economics. Berkshire purchased 84% for a price based on a $55 million valuation.

> "The circumstances of this acquisition were similar to those of Nebraska Furniture Mart: most of the shares were held by people who wished to employ funds elsewhere; family members who enjoyed running their business wanted to continue both as owners and managers."

Buffett notes: "Neither Charlie nor I have been to Cincinnati to see their operation... If our success were to depend upon insights we developed through plant inspections, Berkshire would be in big trouble."

The ad is repeated, with minimum after-tax earnings raised to $10 million.

## Insurance Operations: The Commodity Cycle

Industry combined ratio improved to 108.5 in 1986 (estimated) from 116.5 in 1985, due to premium growth of 22.6% outpacing loss growth of 15.5%. But Buffett warns this is temporary:

> "The rate of gain in the industry's premium volume has slowed significantly... and we expect further slowing in 1987. Indeed, the rate of gain may well fall below my 10% 'equilibrium' figure by the third quarter."

The commodity nature of insurance: capacity expands when profits appear, prices fall. "What's needed is not more statesmen, but less corn."

Berkshire's combined ratio fell from 111 to 103. Premium growth was exceptional – probably the fastest among the top 100 insurers over the past two years. But volume swings: NICO's monthly volume went from $5M in late 1984 to $35M in early 1986, now down to $20M. "C'est la guerre."

**Loss reserving:** Three years in a row of error. "If the physiological rules that applied to Pinocchio were to apply to me, my nose would now draw crowds." "Reserve strengthening" is a euphemism for "correction of previous untruths." Woody Allen: "While the lamb may lie down with the lion, the lamb shouldn't count on getting a whole lot of sleep."

**GEICO:** 41% owned. Outstanding year. Combined ratio 96.9. Premium volume up 16%. Repurchased 5.5% of shares. "Rock-bottom operating costs" – total expense ratio 23.5% vs. many competitors 15 points higher. Bill Snyder "continually widens the moat." Lou Simpson's investment performance vs. S&P 500 is shown – he beat the S&P in six of seven years. Buffett: "It's a little embarrassing for me... Only my ownership of a controlling block of Berkshire stock makes me secure enough to give you the following figures."

## Marketable Securities: Fear and Greed

Buffett purchased $700 million of tax-exempt bonds – "mediocre investments" that "seemed the least objectionable alternative." He lists five directions for money: common stocks, long-term bonds, medium-term bonds, short-term cash, arbitrage. Currently, no equities meet tests.

> "Occasional outbreaks of those two super-contagious diseases, fear and greed, will forever occur in the investment community... We simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful."

As this is written, "little fear is visible in Wall Street. Instead, euphoria prevails." But "stocks can't outperform businesses indefinitely."

Long-term bonds are unattractive due to inflation fears. Arbitrage is a short-term parking place. Medium-term tax-exempts are an alternative to Treasury Bills.

Equity portfolio: Capital Cities/ABC $801.7M, GEICO $674.7M, Washington Post $269.5M. "We expect to keep permanently our three primary holdings."

> "This attitude may seem old-fashioned in a corporate world in which activity has become the order of the day... Investment managers are even more hyperkinetic: their behavior during trading hours makes whirling dervishes appear sedated by comparison. Indeed, the term 'institutional investor' is becoming one of those self-contradictions called an oxymoron, comparable to 'jumbo shrimp,' 'lady mudwrestler' and 'inexpensive lawyer.'"

## NHP, Inc.: A Unique Business

50% owned. Developer, syndicator, owner, and manager of multi-family rental housing. Genealogy: created by a Johnson-appointed commission led by Edgar Kaiser. Over 260 major corporations invested $42 million. Now oversees 500 properties, 80,000 units, $2.5 billion cost. Tax-oriented syndication halted by Tax Reform Act of 1986. Large shareholders: Weyerhauser (25%), management group (Rod Heller).

## Taxation: The Tax Reform Act of 1986

Buffett provides a detailed analysis. Key points:
- Corporate rate decreases from 46% to 34% – positive for Berkshire and investees.
- Corporate capital gains rate increases from 28% to 34% – negative.
- Dividend/interest income taxed more heavily for insurers.
- Loss reserve discounting and unearned premium inclusion accelerate tax payments.
- Fresh start adjustment: one-time benefit of $30-40 million.
- General Utilities Doctrine repealed: double tax on corporate liquidations.

Net effect: negative for Berkshire. "Our rate of increase in business value is likely to be at least moderately slower under the new law than under the old." For shareholders: every dollar of gain produces 72 cents after-tax rather than 80 cents.

Buffett also explains the "pipeline" theory of corporate taxes: in some cases, benefits fall on the corporation; in others, they pass to customers. It depends on the strength of the franchise and whether profits are regulated. For strong franchises, tax cuts benefit the corporation. "If you are tempted to believe otherwise, think for a moment of the most able brain surgeon or lawyer in your area. Do you really expect the fees of this expert... to be reduced now that the top personal tax rate is being cut from 50% to 28%?"

## Miscellaneous

- Corporate jet purchase: Buffett admits he made "intemperate remarks" about corporate jets in the past. "I was forced into my Galileo mode. I promptly experienced the necessary 'counter-revelation' and travel is now considerably easier – and considerably costlier." Ben Franklin: "So convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature, since it enables one to find or make a reason for everything one has a mind to do."
- Shareholder contributions: 97% participation, $4 million to 1,934 charities.
- Annual meeting May 19 in Omaha. 450 attendees. Mrs. B and Oriental rugs: "Unless our shareholders top last year's record, I'll be in trouble."

## The Appendix: Purchase-Price Accounting and the "Cash Flow" Fallacy

This is one of the most important theoretical pieces Buffett ever wrote. It uses Scott Fetzer as an example to show how purchase accounting creates two different sets of earnings for the same business.

**Company O (Old) vs. Company N (New):** Identical economics, different accounting. The "O" column shows what Scott Fetzer would have earned if not purchased. The "N" column shows actual reported earnings. The difference: $11.6 million of non-cash charges from purchase-price adjustments.

**The charges:**
1. Non-cash inventory costs: $4,979,000
2. Extra depreciation: $5,054,000
3. Goodwill amortization: $595,000
4. Deferred-tax acrobatics: $998,000

None are deductible for tax purposes. The "new" Scott Fetzer pays exactly the same tax as the "old" would have.

**Owner earnings:** Buffett defines it as (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges, less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment required to maintain long-term competitive position and unit volume.

> "Our owner-earnings equation does not yield the deceptively precise figures provided by GAAP... We consider the owner earnings figure, not the GAAP figure, to be the relevant item for valuation purposes – both for investors in buying stocks and for managers in buying entire businesses. We agree with Keynes's observation: 'I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong.'"

For Scott Fetzer, (c) is close to (b) on an historical-cost basis, so owner earnings are far better depicted by the O column than the N column.

**The "cash flow" fallacy:** Wall Street reports often present (a) + (b) without subtracting (c). This is deceptive. "Cash flow" is meaningless in manufacturing, retailing, extractive companies, and utilities because (c) is always significant. Only in certain real estate or bridge-like businesses is it useful.

> "To sum up: in the case of both Scott Fetzer and our other businesses, we feel that (b) on an historical-cost basis... is quite close in amount to (c)... Our conviction about this point is the reason we show our amortization and other purchase-price adjustment items separately."

> "Questioning GAAP figures may seem impious to some... But the accountants' job is to record, not to evaluate. The evaluation job falls to investors and managers. Accounting numbers, of course, are the language of business... Managers and owners need to remember, however, that accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1986 Edition)

**1. He distinguishes between accounting earnings and owner earnings.**
The appendix is a masterclass. GAAP can obscure economic reality. Owner earnings – adjusted for non-cash charges and required capital expenditures – is what matters for valuation.

**2. He is brutally honest about his own performance.**
"I was unable to skillfully deploy much of the capital they generated." He takes the blame for capital allocation, while praising the managers for operating results.

**3. He thinks in terms of business economics, not market prices.**
The insurance cycle, the commodity nature of the industry, the "less corn" metaphor – he understands the structural forces that drive profitability.

**4. He values character and integrity above all.**
The Ogilvy quote, the Catholic tailor joke, the "marrying for money" analogy – he wants to work with people he likes and admires. Life is too short to do otherwise.

**5. He is patient and disciplined.**
No new equities, no big acquisitions – but he doesn't force it. He pays off debt and stockpiles funds, waiting for the right opportunity. "We will continue to look for operating businesses that meet our tests and, with luck, will acquire such a business every couple of years."

**6. He has a sense of humor about himself.**
The Pinocchio nose, the corporate jet Galileo mode, the "institutional investor" oxymoron, the Mrs. B jokes – Buffett uses humor to make serious points and to connect with shareholders.

**7. He is a fierce critic of Wall Street practices.**
"Cash flow" numbers, "reserve strengthening" euphemisms, hyperkinetic investment managers – he sees through the nonsense and explains it clearly.

## The Key Quote

> "Our owner-earnings equation does not yield the deceptively precise figures provided by GAAP... We consider the owner earnings figure, not the GAAP figure, to be the relevant item for valuation purposes."

This is the heart of the 1986 letter. Buffett is teaching shareholders how to think about business value. GAAP is a starting point, not an ending point. Owner earnings – adjusted for the real costs of maintaining the business – is what matters.

But the quote that best captures Buffett's philosophy of management and life:

> "We intend to continue our practice of working only with people whom we like and admire. This policy not only maximizes our chances for good results, it also ensures us an extraordinarily good time."

That's the secret. He's not just building a fortune; he's building a life. And he wants the people around him to be people he enjoys. That's why Berkshire is a joy for him – and why shareholders benefit.

## Transition Note

1986 was a year of consolidation. No big acquisitions, but Scott Fetzer and Fechheimer joined the family. The insurance cycle was peaking, but Buffett warned of the coming downturn. The Tax Reform Act created headwinds. And the appendix on owner earnings became one of the most important documents in investment literature.

Buffett was building the intellectual framework that would guide Berkshire for decades. The emphasis on owner earnings, the skepticism of GAAP, the focus on capital allocation, the love of great managers – all of it was crystallizing. The 1986 letter is a masterclass in how to think about business value.
