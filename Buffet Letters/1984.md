# Analysis of the 1985 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (March 4, 1986)

## The Performance: A Freak Year

Net worth gain: **$613.6 million** or **48.2%** – a stunning result. Book value per share grew from $19.46 (1964) to **$1,643.71** (1985) – **23.2% compounded annually** over 21 years.

> "It is fitting that the visit of Halley's Comet coincided with this percentage gain: neither will be seen again in my lifetime."

Buffett immediately warns that nothing close to this rate is achievable in the future. Two factors:

1. **A market offering very little opportunity** compared to 1964-1984. "Today we cannot find significantly-undervalued equities to purchase for our insurance company portfolios."
2. **Size.** Equity capital is more than twenty times what it was ten years ago. "An iron law of business is that growth eventually dampens exceptional economics."

To earn 15% annually over the next ten years now requires profits of **$5.7 billion** – a 48% increase from the $3.9 billion figure of a year earlier. "Leaving aside oil companies, only about 15 U.S. businesses have managed to earn over $5.7 billion during the past ten years."

## The Market Value vs. Business Value Problem

Historically, Berkshire shares sold at a modest discount to intrinsic business value. That discount has disappeared, and occasionally a premium prevails. This is bad news for new owners:

> "If the financial experience of new owners of Berkshire is merely to match the future financial experience of the company, any premium of market value over intrinsic business value that they pay must be maintained."

Buffett prefers a market price that consistently approximates business value. Wild swings cause inequitable distribution of gains among owners.

> "Over the long term there has been a more consistent relationship between Berkshire's market value and business value than has existed for any other publicly-traded equity with which I am familiar. This is a tribute to you."

## The Graham Oil Prospector Story

Buffett quotes Ben Graham's famous parable:

An oil prospector arrives at heaven. St. Peter says the oil men's compound is full. The prospector yells, "Oil discovered in hell." All the oil men rush out. St. Peter invites him in. The prospector pauses: "No, I think I'll go along with the rest of the boys. There might be some truth to that rumor after all."

The lesson: institutional investors, despite their large staffs and experience, often behave like the oil men – following the crowd rather than independent thought.

> "Stocks heavily owned and constantly monitored by institutions have often been among the most inappropriately valued."

> "Security profits in a given year bear similarities to a college graduation ceremony in which the knowledge gained over four years is recognized on a day when nothing further is learned."

## Shutdown of the Textile Business: A Landmark Event

After 21 years, Buffett closed the textile operation. This is one of the most instructive sections in all his letters.

**The history:** When Buffett Partnership bought control in 1965, Berkshire had $22 million of net worth, all in textiles. Intrinsic value was considerably less. In the previous nine years, aggregate sales of $530 million had produced an aggregate loss of $10 million.

**The hope:** Southern non-union plants seemed to have a competitive advantage. Ken Chace was made president. "In this respect we were 100% correct: Ken and his recent successor, Garry Morrison, have been excellent managers, every bit the equal of managers at our more profitable businesses."

**The diversification:** In 1967, textile cash funded the purchase of National Indemnity. "This pullback proved wise."

**The four reasons for staying** (from the 1978 letter): important employers, honest management, cooperative labor, modest cash returns expected.

> "It turned out that I was very wrong about (4). Though 1979 was moderately profitable, the business thereafter consumed major amounts of cash."

**The failed acquisition:** Waumbec Mills was bought expecting "important synergy (a term widely used in business to explain an acquisition that otherwise makes no sense)." Nothing worked.

> "I should be faulted for not quitting sooner. A recent Business Week article stated that 250 textile mills have closed since 1980. Their owners were not privy to any information that was unknown to me; they simply processed it more objectively. I ignored Comte's advice – 'the intellect should be the servant of the heart, but not its slave' – and believed what I preferred to believe."

**The capital investment trap:** Each proposal to invest in textiles looked like an immediate winner. But:

> "Viewed individually, each company's capital investment decision appeared cost-effective and rational; viewed collectively, the decisions neutralized each other and were irrational (just as happens when each person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on tiptoes)."

**The Burlington Industries comparison:** Burlington, the largest U.S. textile company, made $3 billion in capital expenditures over the period. Its stock, adjusted for splits, now sells at just over its 1964 price – while the CPI has tripled. Each share commands about one-third the purchasing power it did in 1964.

> "This devastating outcome for the shareholders indicates what can happen when much brain power and energy are applied to a faulty premise."

**The Samuel Johnson horse:** "A horse that can count to ten is a remarkable horse – not a remarkable mathematician." Likewise, a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable textile company – but not a remarkable business.

> "My conclusion from my own experiences and from much observation of other businesses is that a good managerial record (measured by economic returns) is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row."

> "Should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks."

**The auction postscript:** The textile equipment originally cost about $13 million, had a book value of $866,000, and could have been replaced new for perhaps $30-$50 million. Gross proceeds: **$163,122**. Net: less than zero.

> "Relatively modern looms that we bought for $5,000 apiece in 1981 found no takers at $50. We finally sold them for scrap at $26 each, a sum less than removal costs."

> "Ponder this: the economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo – or a single See's candy store – considerably exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of tangible assets that not too many years ago... was able to employ over 1,000 people."

## Three Very Good Businesses (and a Few Thoughts About Incentive Compensation)

Buffett merges discussion of Nebraska Furniture Mart, See's Candy, and Buffalo Evening News. In 1985 they earned an aggregate of **$72 million pre-tax**. Fifteen years earlier, before acquisition, aggregate earnings were about **$8 million pre-tax**.

**The key insight:** The increase in earnings was not accompanied by a proportional increase in capital. "Although annual earnings are now $64 million greater, the businesses require only about $40 million more in invested capital to operate than was the case then."

> "The average American business has required about $5 of additional capital to generate an additional $1 of annual pre-tax earnings. That business, therefore, would have required over $300 million in additional capital from its owners in order to achieve an earnings performance equal to our group of three."

**The savings account analogy:** Quadruple the capital in a savings account and you quadruple earnings. "You would hardly expect hosannas for that particular accomplishment. Yet, retirement announcements regularly sing the praises of CEOs who have, say, quadrupled earnings of their widget company during their reign – with no one examining whether this gain was attributable simply to many years of retained earnings and the workings of compound interest."

**The stock option critique:** This is one of Buffett's most thorough treatments.

The savings account example: A trustee manages a $100,000 savings account earning 8%. He sets the payout ratio at one-quarter of annual earnings. After ten years, the account is worth $179,084. Earnings increased 70%. Dividends increased from $2,000 to $3,378. All charts would march skyward.

Now give the trustee a ten-year fixed-price option on part of the account. "With such an option, your manager would reap a substantial profit at your expense – just from having held on to most of your earnings."

> "Many stock options in the corporate world have worked in exactly that fashion: they have gained in value simply because management retained earnings, not because it did well with the capital in its hands."

**The double standard:** "Nowhere in the business world are ten-year fixed-price options on all or a portion of a business granted to outsiders. Ten months, in fact, would be regarded as extreme." But managers routinely grant such options to themselves.

> "The unwillingness of managers to do-unto-outsiders, however, is not matched by an unwillingness to do-unto-themselves. (Negotiating with one's self seldom produces a barroom brawl.)"

**The Chrysler example:** The U.S. Government received options on Chrysler shares. When they worked out well, Chrysler sought to modify the payoff, arguing the rewards were outsize. "That anguish may well be unique: to my knowledge, no managers – anywhere – have been similarly offended by unwarranted payoffs arising from options granted to themselves or their colleagues."

**Options and capital costs:** "No owner has ever escaped the burden of capital costs, whereas a holder of a fixed-price option bears no capital costs at all. An owner must weigh upside potential against downside risk; an option holder has no downside."

**Three points about options:**
1. Options should be awarded only to those with overall responsibility. "The .350 hitter expects, and also deserves, a big payoff for his performance – even if he plays for a cellar-dwelling team. And the .150 hitter should get no reward – even if he plays for a pennant winner."
2. Options should have built-in retained-earnings or carrying-cost factors. "Options should be priced at true business value."
3. Some managers Buffett admires disagree. "If it ain't broke, don't fix it" is preferable to "purity at any price."

**Berkshire's system:** Rewards key managers for meeting targets in their own bailiwicks. "If See's does well, that does not produce incentive compensation at the News – nor vice versa. Neither do we look at the price of Berkshire stock when we write bonus checks."

Bonuses can be large – five times base salary, or more. "We do not put a cap on bonuses, and the potential for rewards is not hierarchical." A 20-year-old who can hit .300 is as valuable as a 40-year-old performing as well.

## Insurance Operations: The Industry Cycle

Industry combined ratio: 118.0 in 1985 (estimated). Premiums grew 20.9%. But losses accelerated – up 17% in 1985, exceeding inflation by over 13 percentage points.

**The causes:** Reserve additions ("shouting 'I've sinned, I've sinned'") and "social" or "judicial" inflation. "The insurer's ability to pay has assumed overwhelming importance with juries and judges."

**The short-term outlook:** Good. Premium growth improved as 1985 went along. Combined ratio should fall sharply in 1986.

**The long-term outlook:** "The profit improvement, however, is likely to be of short duration." Two economic principles:
1. Commodity businesses achieve good profitability only when prices are fixed or capacity is short.
2. Managers quickly add capacity when prospects improve and capital is available.

**The insurance commodity problem:** "The typical policyholder does not differentiate between products but concentrates instead on price." The cartel-like procedure that kept prices up has disappeared.

**Capacity is attitudinal, not physical:** "Insurance managers can write whatever amount of business they feel comfortable writing." Capital can be secured instantly. In 1985, about 15 insurers raised well over $3 billion. "If capacity additions continue at this rate, it won't be long before serious price-cutting appears."

**Berkshire's advantage:** "In past reports, I have told you that Berkshire's strong capital position – the best in the industry – should one day allow us to claim a distinct competitive advantage. With the tightening of the market, that day arrived. Our premium volume more than tripled last year."

**The flight to quality:** "A policy is only an IOU – and... in 1985, [buyers] could not collect on many of their IOUs."

**The lay-it-off-at-a-profit game:** Issuing companies kept a small net line while passing most risk to reinsurers. "By doing so, the issuing companies sometimes made money on business that was distinctly unprofitable for the issuing and reinsuring companies combined."

The example: One company wrote $6 billion, kept $2.5 billion (40%), gave $3.5 billion to reinsurers. On the part kept, underwriting loss was less than $200 million. The part laid off produced a loss of over $1.5 billion for reinsurers.

> "Mark Twain's cat: having once sat on a hot stove, it never did so again – but it never again sat on a cold stove, either."

**Berkshire's approach:** "We have never played the lay-it-off-at-a-profit game... Now the tables are turned: we have the underwriting capability whereas others do not."

> "We are perfectly willing to risk losing $10 million of our own money on a single event, as long as we believe that the price is right."

National Indemnity ran an ad soliciting policies with a minimum premium of $1 million. It drew 600 replies and produced premiums totaling about $50 million. "Hold the applause: it's all long-tail business and it will be at least five years before we know whether this marketing success was also an underwriting success."

**Loss reserving:** Combined ratio improved from 134 to 111 but "continues to reflect past misdeeds." Underreserving revealed in 1984 "occurred in the reinsurance area."

**The "except for" story:** The Chairman of General Reinsurance suggested forming the "Except-For Insurance Company" for all the business they later wouldn't want to count.

> "In any business, insurance or otherwise, 'except for' should be excised from the lexicon. If you are going to play the game, you must count the runs scored against you in all nine innings."

**GEICO:** 38%-owned. Excellent premium growth and investment results, but poor underwriting by its lofty standards. Jack Byrne left to head Fireman's Fund. "Jack's performance in reviving GEICO from near-bankruptcy was truly extraordinary... We owe him a great deal."

> "We are equally indebted to Jack for an achievement that eludes most outstanding leaders: he found managers to succeed him who have talents as valuable as his own."

## Fireman's Fund Quota-Share Contract

On September 1, 1985, Berkshire became a 7% participant in all of FFIC's business in force (except reinsurance for unaffiliated companies). Contract runs four years. FFIC remits premiums; Berkshire reimburses for expenses and losses.

Funds generated are held by Berkshire for investment. Buffett is available for consultation on general investment strategy but not involved in specific decisions or underwriting.

FFIC transferred $92.7 million of unearned premium reserve to Berkshire. Berkshire paid $29.4 million for underwriting expenses. Two-sevenths goes to Wesco-Financial Insurance Company (Wes-FIC).

> "After the end of the year, we secured another quota-share contract, whose 1986 volume should be over $50 million."

## Marketable Securities

The portfolio: cost $275 million, market $1.198 billion. Major holdings:
- GEICO: $595.9 million market (cost $45.7 million)
- Washington Post: $205.2 million (cost $9.7 million)
- ABC: $109.0 million (cost $54.4 million)
- Beatrice: $108.1 million (short-term arbitrage)
- Affiliated Publications: $55.7 million (cost $3.5 million)

**The Washington Post story:** Bought in mid-1973 at not more than one-fourth of per-share business value. By yearend 1974, showed a loss of about 25% – market value $8 million against cost of $10.6 million.

> "What we had thought ridiculously cheap a year earlier had become a good bit cheaper as the market, in its infinite wisdom, marked WPC stock down to well below 20 cents on the dollar of intrinsic value."

**The triple dip:** Business value soared; per-share business value increased faster due to stock repurchases; stock price outpaced per-share business value as the discount narrowed.

> "If we had invested our $10.6 million in any of a half-dozen media companies that were investment favorites in mid-1973, the value of our holdings at yearend would have been in the area of $40 - $60 million... The extra $160 million or so we gained through ownership of WPC came, in very large part, from the superior nature of the managerial decisions made by Kay as compared to those made by managers of most media companies."

**The academics:** "Most institutional investors in the early 1970s... regarded business value as of only minor relevance... These institutions were then under the spell of academics at prestigious business schools who were preaching a newly-fashioned theory: the stock market was totally efficient, and therefore calculations of business value – and even thought, itself – were of no importance."

> "We are enormously indebted to those academics: what could be more advantageous in an intellectual contest – whether it be bridge, chess, or stock selection – than to have opponents who have been taught that thinking is a waste of energy?"

**Beatrice:** "A short-term arbitrage holding – in effect, a parking place for money... We would be a lot happier if the funds currently employed on this short-term basis found a long-term home. At the moment, however, prospects are bleak."

**WPPSS bonds:** $194 million at amortized cost. Unrealized gain of $62 million. Annual tax-exempt income about $30 million.

## Capital Cities/ABC

Berkshire purchased 3 million shares at $172.50 per share, helping finance the $3.5 billion acquisition of ABC.

> "I've been on record for many years about the management of Cap Cities: I think it is the best of any publicly-owned company in the country. And Tom Murphy and Dan Burke are not only great managers, they are precisely the sort of fellows that you would want your daughter to marry."

**The unusual agreement:** Tom Murphy (or Dan Burke) votes Berkshire's stock. "This arrangement was initiated by Charlie and me, not by Tom."

**The restrictions on sale:** The block cannot be sold to anyone who is a large holder (or intends to become one) without management approval.

> "Since large blocks frequently command premium prices, some might think we have injured Berkshire financially by creating such restrictions. Our view is just the opposite... the first-class managers with whom we have aligned ourselves can focus their efforts entirely upon running the businesses."

**Corporate instability:** "At any time a major holder can surface, usually mouthing reassuring rhetoric but frequently harboring uncivil intentions."

**The human side:** "We don't want managers we like and admire... to ever lose any sleep wondering whether surprises might occur because of our large ownership. I have told them there will be no surprises, and these agreements put Berkshire's signature where my mouth is."

**The purchase price:** "Our Cap Cities purchase was made at a full price, reflecting the very considerable enthusiasm for both media stocks and media properties."

**The self-deprecating joke:** "Some of you probably wonder why we are now buying Cap Cities at $172.50 per share given that your Chairman... sold Berkshire's holdings in the same company at $43 per share in 1978-80. Anticipating your question, I spent much of 1985 working on a snappy answer that would reconcile these acts. A little more time, please."

## Acquisition of Scott & Fetzer

Right after yearend, Berkshire acquired The Scott & Fetzer Company for about $320 million (plus $90 million of pre-existing debt).

Sales of about $700 million from 17 businesses. Key products: Kirby home-care systems, Campbell Hausfeld air compressors, Wayne burners and water pumps.

**World Book:** 40% of sales and a bit more of income. "By far the leader in its industry, selling more than twice as many encyclopedia sets annually as its nearest competitor." Buffett has been a fan for 25 years.

**Ralph Schey:** CEO for nine years. "When Ralph took charge, the company had 31 businesses, the result of an acquisition spree in the 1960s. He disposed of many that did not fit... Ralph's operating and capital-allocation record is superb."

**The acquisition story:** Buffett read about the company's odyssey in the newspapers. On October 10, he wrote a short letter to Ralph. They met for dinner on October 22 and signed an acquisition contract the following week.

> "The Scott Fetzer acquisition, plus major growth in our insurance business, should push revenues above $2 billion in 1986, more than double those of 1985."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1985 Edition)

**1. He learns from mistakes and shares the lessons.**
The textile shutdown is a masterclass in capital allocation failure. He admits he ignored Comte's advice and "believed what I preferred to believe." The lesson: "When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."

**2. He thinks in terms of capital efficiency, not earnings growth.**
The three businesses earned $72 million pre-tax on only $40 million of additional capital. That's the power of economic goodwill. Most American businesses need $5 of additional capital for $1 of additional earnings.

**3. He is a fierce critic of stock options.**
The savings account analogy is devastating. Options reward managers for retained earnings, not performance. "A managerial Rip Van Winkle, ready to doze for ten years, could not wish for a better 'incentive' system."

**4. He understands industry cycles and positions Berkshire to profit.**
The insurance industry's capacity is "attitudinal, not physical." When capital floods in, prices collapse. Berkshire's strong capital position allows it to write large net lines when others retreat.

**5. He values managerial character above all.**
Tom Murphy and Dan Burke: "precisely the sort of fellows that you would want your daughter to marry." Kay Graham: "brains and courage." Mrs. B: "continues at age 92 to set a pace at the store that none of us can keep up with."

**6. He structures deals to align interests.**
The Cap Cities agreement (Murphy votes Berkshire's stock, restrictions on sale) ensures stability and allows managers to focus on long-term value.

**7. He is self-deprecating and funny.**
The oil prospector story, the "snappy answer" about selling Cap Cities at $43, the Cherry Coke announcement – Buffett uses humor to make serious points and to connect with shareholders.

## The Key Quote

> "My conclusion from my own experiences and from much observation of other businesses is that a good managerial record (measured by economic returns) is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row."

This is the central lesson of the textile saga. Buffett and his managers rowed brilliantly – but the boat was sinking. No amount of effort could overcome the economics of a commodity business with massive overcapacity. The same effort applied to See's, the News, or NFM produced spectacular results.

But the most important passage for shareholders may be:

> "Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect – backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future – that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners."

That's the test. Berkshire has passed it for 21 years. The question is whether it can continue to pass it with a much larger capital base. Buffett is honest: it will be much harder.

## Transition Note

1985 was a watershed year. The textile business – the original foundation of Berkshire – was finally shut down. The lessons were painful but permanently learned. Capital Cities/ABC and Scott & Fetzer were acquired. The insurance business was positioned for a period of tight capacity.

But the most important legacy of the 1985 letter is the **incentive compensation section**. It is Buffett's most complete critique of stock options and his clearest statement of how managers should be paid. Every board of directors should read it. Every shareholder should understand it.

The Cherry Coke announcement is a light note at the end of a serious letter. But it also signals something important: Buffett is having fun. He loves his work, his managers, and his shareholders. That joy comes through on every page.
