# Analysis of the 1983 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (March 14, 1984)

## The Manager-Owner Relationship: The Catechism

With shareholders growing from 1,900 to 2,900 (largely due to the Blue Chip merger), Buffett lays out the core principles governing the manager-owner relationship. This is one of the clearest statements of Berkshire's philosophy ever written:

- **Partnership attitude:** "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership." Shareholders are owner-partners; Buffett and Munger are managing partners.
- **Skin in the game:** Directors are all major shareholders; at least four of five have over 50% of family net worth in Berkshire. "We eat our own cooking."
- **Per-share progress, not size:** The goal is to maximize average annual gain in intrinsic business value per share. "We do not measure the economic significance or performance of Berkshire by its size."
- **Two-pronged ownership:** Direct ownership of businesses generating cash and above-average returns; second choice is owning parts of similar businesses through marketable stocks.
- **Accounting consequences don't drive decisions:** "We much prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard accounting principles than to purchase $1 of earnings that is reportable."
- **Conservative debt:** "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet."
- **No managerial wish list at shareholder expense:** "We will only do with your money what we would do with our own."
- **Retention test:** Retained earnings must deliver at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained. "To date, this test has been met."
- **Share issuance rule:** "We will issue common stock only when we receive as much in business value as we give."
- **No selling good businesses:** "Regardless of price, we have no interest at all in selling any good businesses that Berkshire owns."
- **Candor in reporting:** "Our guideline is to tell you the business facts that we would want to know if our positions were reversed."
- **Silence on securities:** "Good investment ideas are rare, valuable and subject to competitive appropriation just as good product or business acquisition ideas are."

This is the "catechism" – and it remains essentially unchanged today.

## Nebraska Furniture Mart: The Rose Blumkin Story

This is one of the most beloved passages in all of Buffett's writings. Mrs. B – Rose Blumkin – arrived from Russia at 23 with no education and no English. In 1937, with $500 saved from selling used clothing, she opened a furniture store. She named it after the American Furniture Mart in Chicago.

She faced every obstacle: entrenched competition, manufacturers pressured not to sell to her, Fair Trade lawsuits. She won all the cases and got invaluable publicity. At the end of one case, after demonstrating she could profitably sell carpet at a huge discount, she sold the judge $1,400 worth of carpet.

Today, Nebraska Furniture Mart generates over **$100 million in annual sales from one 200,000-square-foot store** – more than all Omaha competitors combined.

Buffett's appraisal test: "How would I like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it?" His answer: "I'd rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with Mrs. B and her progeny."

> "It's the ideal business – one built upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners."

Buffett bought 90% of the business, leaving 10% with family members involved in management, and optioned 10% to key young family managers. Mrs. B, now 90, remains Chairman and is on the sales floor seven days a week.

> "Even Pascal would have left the room for Mrs. Blumkin."

## Corporate Performance: 22.6% Compounded

Book value per share increased from $737.43 to **$975.83** – a 32% gain for the year. Over 19 years of present management: **22.6% compounded annually**.

> "Considering our present size, nothing close to this rate of return can be sustained. Those who believe otherwise should pursue a career in sales, but avoid one in mathematics."

**Book value vs. intrinsic business value:** Buffett explains the difference with a beautiful analogy. Book value is what has been put in; intrinsic business value is what can be taken out. Two children can receive identical financial inputs (book value) but produce wildly different future payoffs (intrinsic value). At Berkshire in 1965, book value **overstated** intrinsic value (textile assets). Now, intrinsic value **considerably exceeds** book value, for two reasons:
1. Non-insurance equities carried at lower of cost or market – $70 million pre-tax excess not in book value.
2. Economic Goodwill far exceeding accounting Goodwill.

## The Goodwill Appendix: A Masterclass

The appendix is one of Buffett's most important theoretical writings. He explains why economic Goodwill and accounting Goodwill differ enormously.

**The See's Candy example:** Blue Chip bought See's in 1972 for $25 million when See's had $8 million in net tangible assets. The $17 million excess was accounting Goodwill, amortized at $425,000 annually for 40 years.

By 1983, after 11 years, the accounting Goodwill had been reduced to about $12.5 million. But See's was earning **$13 million after taxes on about $20 million of net tangible assets** – indicating economic Goodwill far larger than the original cost.

> "While accounting Goodwill regularly decreased from the moment of purchase, economic Goodwill increased in irregular but very substantial fashion."

**The inflation insight:** Economic Goodwill tends to rise with inflation. A business needing few tangible assets (like See's) suffers least from inflation's demand for additional capital. A mundane business needing $18 million in tangible assets to earn $2 million suffers far more.

> "During inflation, Goodwill is the gift that keeps giving."

**The spurious Goodwill problem:** When a management overpays for a business, the silliness ends up in the Goodwill account. Buffett suggests it might better be labeled "No-Will."

**The two perspectives:**
1. In analyzing operating results, amortization charges should be ignored. Focus on earnings on unleveraged net tangible assets.
2. In evaluating acquisitions, amortization charges should be ignored. View purchased Goodwill at full cost, including the full intrinsic business value of consideration given.

> "A good business is not always a good purchase – although it's a good place to look for one."

## Sources of Earnings: Insurance Collapse

The table shows the damage:
- Insurance underwriting: **($33.9 million)** pre-tax – worse than 1982
- Net investment income: $43.8 million (up from $41.6 million)
- See's Candies: $24.5 million (up from $14.2 million)
- Buffalo Evening News: **$16.5 million** (up from a loss of $0.7 million) – the turnaround!
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: $3.0 million (partial year)
- Special GEICO distribution: $21.0 million

Berkshire's share of operating earnings: **$72.4 million** pre-tax, up from $27.7 million.

## Buffalo Evening News: The Turnaround

The News exceeded its 10% after-tax profit target. Two factors: a state income tax loss carry-forward and a fluke drop in newsprint costs.

**Penetration ratio:** The News ranks **number one in weekday penetration among the 100 largest U.S. papers** – and number three in Sunday penetration.

Buffett explains three reasons:
1. Stable population (low emigration/immigration) – more interested in local news.
2. Editorial quality and integrity, honed by Alfred Kirchhofer and preserved by Murray Light.
3. News hole of 50% (vs. high 30s average) – 25% more news than typical papers.

> "This news-rich mixture is by intent. Some publishers, pushing for higher profit margins, have cut their news holes during the past decade. We have maintained ours and will continue to do so."

## See's Candy: Volume Problems, Exceptional Economics

See's strengths are many:
- Candy preferred by enormous margin in the West
- Customer service second to none
- 2,000 seasonal workers, cheerful and helpful

But volume is flat: poundage down 0.8% on a same-store basis in 1983, cumulative decline of about 8% since 1979. Quantity-order volume (25% of total) has plateaued. Price increases have driven dollar volume, but unit volume is the key measure.

> "In candy, as in stocks, price and value can differ; price is what you give, value is what you get."

## Insurance – Controlled Operations: Terrible Results

Buffett is brutally honest:

> "In aggregate, the companies we operate and whose underwriting results reflect the consequences of decisions that were my responsibility a few years ago, had absolutely terrible results. Fortunately, GEICO, whose policies I do not influence, simply shot the lights out. The inference you draw from this summary is the correct one. I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost."

Berkshire's combined ratio: **121** vs. industry 111. The roots: "operating and personnel decisions made two or more years back when I had direct managerial responsibility for the insurance group."

But several managers did outstanding jobs: Roland Miller, Tom Rowley.

**New initiatives:** Structured settlements and assumption of loss reserves – where Berkshire's financial strength is the key selling point. "No other insurer we know of – even those with much larger gross assets – has our financial strength."

## Insurance – GEICO: Shot the Lights Out

GEICO's combined ratio: **96** vs. industry 111.

> "A few years ago I would not have thought GEICO could so greatly outperform the industry. Its superiority reflects the combination of a truly exceptional business idea and an exceptional management."

Jack Byrne, Bill Snyder, and Lou Simpson – "the three of them are some team."

Berkshire's ~1/3 interest gives it a **$270 million share of GEICO's premium volume** – 80% larger than Berkshire's own volume. "The major portion of our total insurance business comes from the best insurance book in the country."

## Stock Splits: The Pickpocket of Enterprise

This is one of Buffett's most famous sections. He explains why Berkshire will not split its stock.

**The goal:** Have Berkshire stock sell at a price "rationally related to its intrinsic business value." The key is rational shareholders, both current and prospective.

> "Manic-depressive personalities produce manic-depressive valuations."

**Self-selection:** Like a musical event advertised as opera vs. rock concert, Berkshire's communications attract a certain type of shareholder. "We want those who think of themselves as business owners and invest in companies with the intention of staying a long time."

Over 90% (probably 95%) of shares are held by those who were shareholders five years ago. Over 95% hold Berkshire as at least double their next largest holding.

> "Were we to split the stock... we would attract an entering class of buyers inferior to the exiting class of sellers."

**The stock market activity problem:**

> "Brokers, using terms such as 'marketability' and 'liquidity', sing the praises of companies with high share turnover (those who cannot fill your pocket will confidently fill your ear). But investors should understand that what is good for the croupier is not good for the customer. A hyperactive stock market is the pickpocket of enterprise."

**The chair-changing tax:** A company earning 12% on equity with 100% turnover and 1% commissions on each side means owners pay 2% of net worth annually for the privilege of transferring ownership. "1/6 of [earnings] are lost to the owners through the 'frictional' cost of transfer."

If 100 million-share days persist for a year and average cost is 15 cents a share, the "chair-changing tax" totals about **$7.5 billion** – roughly the combined 1982 profits of Exxon, GM, Mobil and Texaco.

> "These expensive activities may decide who eats the pie, but they don't enlarge it."

**The invisible foot:**

> "Adam Smith felt that all noncollusive acts in a free market were guided by an invisible hand that led an economy to maximum progress; our view is that casino-type markets and hair-trigger investment management act as an invisible foot that trips up and slows down a forward-moving economy."

Berkshire's turnover: ~3% per year, with spread ~2%. Annual cost to owners: perhaps 6/100 of 1% of market value, about $900,000. Splitting would increase that cost and downgrade shareholder quality.

## Miscellaneous

- Acquisition criteria repeated: large purchases ($5M+ after-tax), consistent earning power, good ROE with little debt, management in place, simple businesses, offering price.
- Charitable contributions: 96.4% of eligible shares participated; $3,066,501 to 1,353 charities.
- Blue Chip merger: less than 0.1% voted against; no appraisal requests. Berkshire now has 1,146,909 shares vs. 1,137,778 at start of fiscal 1965 – for every 1% owned then, you now own .99%. "All of today's assets... have been added to the original textile assets with virtually no net dilution."

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1983 Edition)

**1. He sees accounting as a language, not reality.**
The Goodwill appendix is a masterclass. Accounting Goodwill decreases while economic Goodwill increases. He reads the language and translates.

**2. He thinks in terms of inflation-adjusted, after-tax, real returns.**
The Goodwill analysis shows why asset-light, franchise-heavy businesses win during inflation. See's needs only $8 million in additional capital to double earnings; a mundane business needs $18 million.

**3. He values owners over traders.**
The stock split section is a profound statement of philosophy. He wants owners who think like owners, not traders who think like gamblers. "A hyperactive stock market is the pickpocket of enterprise."

**4. He takes full blame for mistakes.**
"I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost." No excuses. The insurance results were his fault.

**5. He celebrates character and old age.**
Mrs. B at 90, still selling carpet seven days a week. "Even Pascal would have left the room for Mrs. Blumkin." He values energy, work ethic, and integrity above all.

**6. He understands that quality of shareholders is self-selected.**
Through communications and policies, Berkshire attracts the right kind of owners. "Shareholder eugenics" may be impossible, but self-selection works.

**7. He is willing to be unconventional.**
No stock splits. No dividends. No forecasts. No Wall Street glad-handing. He runs Berkshire for owners who think like him.

## The Key Quote

> "We will issue common stock only when we receive as much in business value as we give."

This is the shareholder's golden rule. It seems obvious. It is violated constantly. And it explains why Berkshire's shareholders have been so well served.

But the most important passage may be this:

> "Managers and investors alike must understand that accounting numbers are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation."

That is the thread that runs through everything – the non-controlled earnings, the Goodwill appendix, the share issuance analysis. Accounting is a language, not reality. Buffett reads the language and translates it into economic truth.

## Transition Note

1983 was a pivotal year. The Blue Chip merger simplified the structure. Nebraska Furniture Mart joined the family. The Buffalo News finally turned a profit. See's continued to print money. GEICO was a rocket.

But the insurance underwriting was terrible – Buffett's fault. And the stock split section laid down a marker: Berkshire would not play the Wall Street game.

The Goodwill appendix is perhaps the single most important theoretical document Buffett ever wrote. It explains why Berkshire's intrinsic value far exceeds book value – and why the accounting numbers that Wall Street focuses on are often misleading.

The seeds of the future were all there: the owner-oriented philosophy, the focus on economic Goodwill, the disdain for hyperactivity, the love of great managers. Buffett was building a cathedral, not a casino.
