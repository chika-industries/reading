# Analysis of the 1979 Berkshire Hathaway Letter

## Accounting Changes and Philosophy

The 1979 letter opens with a new accounting rule: insurance companies must now carry equity securities at **market value** on the balance sheet. Previously, they used lower‑of‑cost‑or‑market. Since Berkshire had large unrealized gains, this change substantially increased reported net worth (after establishing a tax liability).

But Buffett points out a bizarre inconsistency: Blue Chip Stamps (60% owned, fully consolidated) still uses the old lower‑of‑cost‑or‑market rule. So identical equities could be carried at different values in the same consolidated balance sheet – “That should keep you on your toes.”

He makes a crucial distinction:

- **Single‑year operating performance:** Use cost basis for equity values, because market‑value net worth creates a “yo‑yo denominator” that distorts the operating return.
- **Long‑term economic performance:** Use market values (including unrealized gains), because capital appreciation is as important as operating earnings over time.

## 1979 Operating Results: 18.6% Return on Beginning Equity

Operating earnings (excluding securities gains) came to **18.6% of beginning net worth** (with securities valued at cost). That was “reasonably good” but slightly down from 1978.

> “Earnings per share, of course, increased somewhat (about 20%) but we regard this as an improper figure upon which to focus. … ‘Earnings per share’ will rise constantly on a dormant savings account or on a U.S. Savings Bond bearing a fixed rate of return simply because ‘earnings’ are continuously plowed back and added to the capital base. Thus, even a ‘stopped clock’ can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low.”

His primary test: **Return on equity capital** – not growth in EPS.

> “In our view, many businesses would be better understood by their shareholder owners, as well as the general public, if managements and financial analysts modified the primary emphasis they place upon earnings per share, and upon yearly changes in that figure.”

## Long‑Term Results: 20.5% Compounded Since 1964

Book value per share – with equities at market – has grown from $19.46 in 1964 to **$335.85** at yearend 1979. That’s a **20.5% compounded annual rate** – far higher than the operating returns, reflecting the huge capital gains from equities.

But Buffett notes that the 1964 book value “somewhat overstated intrinsic value” (assets weren’t worth 100 cents on the dollar), while today’s market‑value book is probably more realistic.

He is proud of the record: low leverage, no share issuances, built through cash acquisitions and internal growth. Yet he immediately pivots to a sobering point.

## The Inflation Warning (Gold Half‑Ounce)

> “A few years ago, a business whose per‑share net worth compounded at 20% annually would have guaranteed its owners a highly successful real investment return. Now such an outcome seems less certain.”

Why? **The investor’s misery index** = inflation rate + tax rate on capital gains/dividends. If that index exceeds the return on equity, real purchasing power shrinks even if you consume nothing.

> “If we should continue to achieve a 20% compounded gain … your after‑tax purchasing power gain is likely to be very close to zero at a 14% inflation rate.”

He gives a vivid example:

> “One friendly but sharp‑eyed commentator on Berkshire has pointed out that our book value at the end of 1964 would have bought about one‑half ounce of gold and, fifteen years later, after we have plowed back all earnings along with much blood, sweat and tears, the book value produced will buy about the same half ounce.”

The government can print money but not gold or oil. Inflation is the most important external factor for shareholders – and Buffett has no corporate solution. High inflation won’t help Berkshire earn higher returns.

## Textiles and Retailing: “Turnarounds Seldom Turn”

Buffett praises Ben Rosner (Associated Retail Stores) – “Year after year, he produces very large earnings relative to capital employed … Ben is now 76.” But textiles are a disappointment.

He admits a major mistake: buying Waumbec Mills in Manchester, New Hampshire. Even though the price was an “extraordinary bargain” – below working capital – the acquisition was a mistake.

> “Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that ‘turnarounds’ seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price.”

This is a landmark statement. Buffett had learned from painful experience that a cheap price for a bad business is not value – it’s a value trap.

## Insurance Underwriting: Phil Liesche Shines, Industry Worsens

The industry combined ratio rose to 100.7% (underwriting loss). Berkshire’s combined ratio actually improved to 97.1% – thanks largely to Phil Liesche’s group, which produced $8.4 million underwriting profit on $82 million premiums, an extraordinary result.

> “We hear a great many insurance managers talk about being willing to reduce volume in order to underwrite profitably, but we find that very few actually do so. Phil Liesche is an exception.”

On the Homestate operation: Cornhusker, historically a winner, had poor results due to data processing and personnel problems. Buffett says, “We have made some major mistakes in reorganizing our data processing activities.”

**Reinsurance outlook:** “Influx of capital into the business … may well produce disastrous results for many entrants.” Berkshire’s reinsurance activity “may decline substantially.”

**Worker’s Compensation:** Milt Thornton at Cypress is a “gem”. Frank DeNardo has straightened out the Los Angeles mess.

**Industry forecast for 1980–84:** Buffett expects average combined ratio of about 105 – worse than past. High interest rates make insurers tolerate underwriting losses to get investment income. “Thus we expect that competition will create a new threshold of tolerance for underwriting losses.”

## The Bond Mistake: A Long‑Term Lender No More

This is one of Buffett’s most candid sections. He admits that Berkshire made a serious error in bond investing – even though they were more cautious than most.

> “It was a mistake to buy fifteen‑year bonds, and yet we did; we made an even more serious mistake in not selling them (at losses, if necessary) when our present views began to crystallize.”

He compares buying long bonds to setting a fixed price for a product for thirty or forty years in an inflationary world – something no sane business would do.

> “How can we be expected to look forward twelve months and estimate such imponderables as hospital costs … but then … take the proceeds from the sale of that six‑month policy [and] sell the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years?”

He concludes: “Neither a short‑term borrower nor a long‑term lender be” – slightly restating Polonius. Berkshire will stick to convertible bonds (which can be converted to stock), not long‑term straight bonds.

## Banking: The Forced Divestiture

The Illinois National Bank must be divested by December 31, 1980, under the Bank Holding Company Act. Buffett had planned a spin‑off, but the Federal Reserve ruled that if the bank is spun off, no officer or director of Berkshire can serve on the bank’s board – even though Buffett would own over 40% of both.

So he is investigating a sale. “We will be most choosy about any purchaser, and our selection will not be based solely on price.” He warns shareholders that the bank’s earning power will not be fully replaced – “You simply can’t buy high quality businesses at the sort of price/earnings multiple likely to prevail on our bank sale.”

## On Shareholder Communication (The Restaurant Analogy)

Buffett explains that Berkshire’s shareholder base is unusual: about 98% of shares are held by the same people year after year. For them, Berkshire is their largest security holding. He writes the annual report as if speaking to owners in a private company.

He quotes Phil Fisher’s restaurant analogy: a restaurant must choose its clientele and stick to it. “You can’t be all things to all men.” Berkshire wants owners who understand and approve of its policies and will stay for years. That is why he gives no narrative with quarterly reports – nothing meaningful changes that often.

> “We don’t expect a public relations document when our operating managers tell us what is going on, and we don’t feel you should receive such a document.”

## Prospects for 1980

He expects operating return on equity to decline from 18.6%, and there is “a fair chance that operating earnings in aggregate dollars will fall short of 1979.” The outcome depends on the bank disposition, insurance underwriting slippage, and problems in the savings and loan industry.

## How Buffett’s Mind Works (1979 Edition)

**1. He thinks in real (inflation‑adjusted) terms, not nominal.**
The gold half‑ounce metaphor is devastating. After fifteen years of brilliant management and 20% compounding, the real purchasing power has gone nowhere. He is not joking – it is a warning that inflation can destroy equity returns even for great businesses.

**2. He admits mistakes openly and draws lasting lessons.**
Waumbec taught him that “turnarounds seldom turn.” The bond mistake taught him not to be a long‑term lender. He doesn’t hide errors; he uses them to refine his philosophy.

**3. He separates accounting convention from economic reality.**
The inconsistency between insurance and Blue Chip accounting, the problem of using market‑value net worth to compute operating returns – he sees through the rules to the underlying truth.

**4. He values managerial discipline over volume.**
Phil Liesche is praised for reducing premium volume when prices are inadequate. That is rare in the insurance industry.

**5. He treats retained earnings of investees as his own.**
The entire equity portfolio strategy rests on the belief that dollars retained by wonderful businesses will create at least a dollar of market value. That is the core of his “passive ownership” philosophy.

## The Key Quote

> “Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that ‘turnarounds’ seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price.”

This is the mature Buffett. Earlier in his career (Sanborn Map, Dempster) he made money buying poor businesses at bargain prices and forcing change. But Waumbec taught him that the better path is to buy a wonderful business at a fair price. That insight would shape everything from See’s Candy to Coca-Cola to GEICO.

The second essential quote:

> “A business earning 20% on capital can produce a negative real return for its owners under inflationary conditions not much more severe than presently prevail.”

That is the sobering context for all of Berkshire’s future decisions. Even if he performs brilliantly, the external monetary environment may still defeat real wealth creation. He is fighting inflation as much as competitors.
