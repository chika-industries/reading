# Analysis of the 1990 Berkshire Hathaway Letter (March 1, 1991)

## The Performance: 7.3%, and a Prediction Half-Fulfilled

Net worth gain: **$362 million**, or **7.3%**. Over 26 years: $19.46 → **$4,612.06** per share, **23.2% compounded annually**.

Buffett opens by acknowledging he'd predicted a decline: *"A reduction is almost certain in at least one of the next three years."* He was on track to be right through much of the second half — then late-year stock strength rescued the number.

The reason growth was "lackluster": the **permanent four** (Cap Cities/ABC, Coca-Cola, GEICO, Washington Post) showed little aggregate market change. But the composition shifted: the two media stocks fell significantly, Coke rose significantly.

He then does something worth flagging — he explains the arithmetic of shareholder return:

> "If you buy at a 10% premium to intrinsic value; if intrinsic value subsequently grows at 15% a year; and if you then sell at a 10% premium, your own return will correspondingly be 15% compounded... If, however, you buy at a premium and sell at a smaller premium, your results will be somewhat inferior."

Hence the wish for the stock to trade near intrinsic value — not to maximize price, but so that *every* shareholder's return matches the company's.

## The Emily Story: His Actual Job Description

The best short passage in the letter. At his granddaughter's fourth birthday party, Beemer the Clown had Emily wave a wand over "the box of wonders." Green handkerchiefs went in, blue ones came out. Loose handkerchiefs went in, knotted ones emerged. After four transformations:

> "Her face aglow, she exulted: 'Gee, I'm really good at this.'"

> "And that sums up my contribution to the performance of Berkshire's business magicians... They deserve your applause."

This is not false modesty. It's an accurate description of a decentralized capital-allocation model: identify great operators, leave them alone, take the cash they send up, and deploy it.

## Look-Through Earnings: The Framework Formalized

This is the letter where the concept gets a number attached.

**The Cap Cities illustration:** Berkshire's 17% share of Cap Cities earnings was **over $83 million** in 1990. Only about **$530,000** — the after-tax dividend — shows up in Berkshire's GAAP earnings. The residual ~$82 million "stayed with Cap Cities as retained earnings, which work for our benefit but go unrecorded on our books."

> "Our perspective on such 'forgotten-but-not-gone' earnings is simple: The way they are accounted for is of no importance, but their ownership and subsequent utilization is all-important. We care not whether the auditors hear a tree fall in the forest; we do care who owns the tree and what's next done with it."

**The calculation:**
- ~$250 million: Berkshire's share of investee retained operating earnings
- Less ~$30 million: incremental taxes had it been paid as dividends
- Equals ~$220 million
- Plus reported operating earnings of $371 million
- **= ~$590 million in "look-through" earnings**

And the crucial structural point about why retention beats distribution:

> "When Coca-Cola uses retained earnings to repurchase its shares, the company increases our percentage ownership in what I regard to be the most valuable franchise in the world... Instead of repurchasing stock, Coca-Cola could pay those funds to us in dividends, which we could then use to purchase more Coke shares. That would be a less efficient scenario: Because of taxes we would pay on dividend income, we would not be able to increase our proportionate ownership to the degree that Coke can, acting for us."

**The warning:** The Gillette preferred is being converted April 1, reducing reported earnings ~$35 million annually and look-through earnings by a smaller but significant amount. Media earnings — direct and look-through — will decline.

## Non-Insurance Operations: 51% Return, From Prosaic Industries

After-tax earnings on average equity: **51%** — roughly 20th on the Fortune 500. And critically:

> "Leverage did not produce it: Almost all our major facilities are owned, not leased... if the measurement was return on assets... our group would rank in Fortune's top ten."

> "Our return was not earned from industries, such as cigarettes or network television stations, possessing spectacular economics for all participating in them. Instead it came from a group of businesses operating in such prosaic fields as furniture retailing, candy, vacuum cleaners, and even steel warehousing."

**Borsheim's:** Sales up 18% in a poor retail year. Operating costs ~18% of sales vs. ~40% at the typical competitor. And this line, which is the whole low-cost-retailer thesis in six words:

> "What works with diapers works with diamonds."

The mail-order operation is a lovely detail: 1,500 assortments mailed, carrying values from under $1,000 to hundreds of thousands, sent to people no one at Borsheim's has met. "We have yet to experience a loss from customer dishonesty."

**NFM:** $159 million in sales, up 4%. Operating costs 15% vs. Levitz at 40% and Circuit City at 25%. And the merchandising-power proof:

> "A recent report of consumer behavior in Des Moines... showed that NFM was Number 3 in popularity among 20 furniture retailers serving that city. That may sound like no big deal until you consider that 19 of those retailers are located in Des Moines, whereas our store is 130 miles away."

**The synergy counterrevelation:** After years of deriding synergy claims as "the last refuge of scoundrels defending foolish acquisitions," a See's candy cart in NFM "sold more candy than that moved by some of the full-fledged stores See's operates in California."

> "This success contradicts all tenets of retailing. With the Blumkins, though, the impossible is routine."

**See's Albuquerque story:** After 15 years, the landlord refused to renew the lease, wanting them to move to an inferior location at higher rent. The manager, Ann Filkins, on her own initiative, urged customers to protest. **263 responded** with letters and calls, some threatening to boycott the mall. A reporter picked it up. The landlord relented. Chuck Huggins wrote personal thank-you letters with gift certificates and ran a newspaper ad listing all 263 names. Christmas sales rose substantially.

**The capital discipline point:** Net worth of the non-insurance operations rose only $47 million on $133 million of earnings — not because managers skimp on investment, but because:

> "In the past five years they have funneled well over 80% of their earnings to Charlie and me for use in new business and investment opportunities."

## Media: The Most Important Admission in the Letter

This is the section that matters most historically. Buffett had spent decades calling newspapers "economic marvels." Now:

> "The business showed far more vulnerability to the early stages of a recession than has been the case in the past. The question is whether this erosion is just part of an aberrational cycle... or whether the business has slipped in a way that permanently reduces intrinsic business values."

> "Since I didn't predict what has happened, you may question the value of my prediction about what will happen. Nevertheless, I'll proffer a judgment: While many media businesses will remain economic marvels in comparison with American industry generally, they will prove considerably less marvelous than I, the industry, or lenders thought would be the case only a few years ago."

**The diagnosis** — three causes, in ascending order of importance:
1. Advertising dollars growing slowly
2. Retailers that do little or no media advertising gradually taking share in certain categories
3. **"Most important of all, the number of both print and electronic advertising channels has substantially increased. As a consequence, advertising dollars are more widely dispersed and the pricing power of ad vendors has diminished."**

The last point is the permanent one. It's a structural change in supply, not a cyclical dip in demand. And he's explicit that it reduces intrinsic value of both the media investments *and* the Buffalo News.

Yet the product commitment holds: news hole rose to **52.3%** from 50.1% — "alas, the increase resulted from a decline in advertising pages rather than from a gain in news pages."

> "Regardless of earnings pressures, we will maintain at least a 50% news hole. Cutting product quality is not a proper response to adversity."

## Insurance: The Float Cost Table

This is the letter's most original analytical contribution — a table running 1967–1990 showing underwriting loss against average float, producing a **"cost of funds"** for the insurance operation, compared against the yield on long-term government bonds.

**The result: in 19 of 24 years, Berkshire developed funds at a cost below what the government paid.**

1990: $26.65 million underwriting loss on $1,637.3 million average float = **1.63% cost of funds**, vs. 8.24% on long-term Treasuries.

**The two qualifications he insists on:**
1. "The fat lady has yet to gargle, let alone sing" — true cost won't be known until all losses from 1967–1990 are settled decades hence
2. The tax penalty: indirect ownership via an insurer means shareholders pay taxes on investment income that direct ownership would avoid. He estimates this adds about **one percentage point** to the cost of float.

> "Figuring a cost of funds for an insurance business allows anyone analyzing it to determine whether the operation has a positive or negative value for shareholders. If this cost (including the tax penalty) is higher than that applying to alternative sources of funds, the value is negative. If the cost is lower, the value is positive — and if the cost is significantly lower, the insurance business qualifies as a very valuable asset."

And the killer comparison for most of the industry:

> "These companies... are vulnerable to catastrophe losses that could exceed their reinsurance protection and take their cost of float right off the chart. Unless these companies can materially improve their underwriting performance — and history indicates that is an almost impossible task — their shareholders will experience results similar to those borne by the owners of a bank that pays a higher rate of interest on deposits than it receives on loans."

**The measurement critique:** He argues underwriting loss/float is a better metric than the combined ratio, and warns that long-tail lines can look profitable at 110–115 combined while destroying capital:

> "Setting a target of 100 can itself result in heavy losses; aiming for 110–115 is business suicide."

**On his own record:**

> "A particularly encouraging point about our record is that it was achieved despite some colossal mistakes made by your Chairman prior to Mike Goldberg's arrival. Insurance offers a host of opportunities for error, and when opportunity knocked, too often I answered. Many years later, the bills keep arriving for these mistakes: In the insurance business, there is no statute of limitations on stupidity."

## Super-Cats: Lumpy 15% Over Smooth 12%

The structural innovation of the year. Berkshire became a large seller of insurance against major catastrophes, sold to reinsurers who want to lay off exposure.

**The economics are deliberately extreme:**

> "Say... that we take in annual premiums of $100 million from super-cat policies of all kinds. In that case we are very likely in any given year to report either a profit of close to $100 million or a loss of well over $200 million. Note that we are not spreading risk as insurers typically do; we are concentrating it. Therefore, our yearly combined ratio on this business will almost never fall in the industry range of 100–120, but will instead be close to either zero or 300%."

**Why Berkshire can do it and others can't:**

> "Most insurers are financially unable to tolerate such swings. And if they have the ability to do so, they often lack the desire... In addition, most corporate managements believe that their shareholders dislike volatility in results."

> "We can take a different tack... we believe that Berkshire shareholders, if properly informed, can handle unusual volatility in profits so long as the swings carry with them the prospect of superior long-term results. (Charlie and I always have preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%.)"

**The three caveats, stated plainly:**
1. Over a decade, satisfactory; in an occasional year, "absolutely terrible"
2. Expectations rest on "little more than subjective judgments" — historical loss data are of limited value for pricing today
3. They'll only write at prices commensurate with risk; if competitors get optimistic, volume falls

> "At the moment, we believe Berkshire to be the largest U.S. writer of super-cat business. So when a major quake occurs in an urban area or a winter storm rages across Europe, light a candle for us."

## Wells Fargo: Buying Into a Panic

Berkshire took its stake to just under **10%** (the most it can own without Fed approval), paying **$290 million** — "less than five times after-tax earnings, and less than three times pre-tax earnings."

The setup is the classic panic: month by month, "the foolish loan decisions of once well-regarded banks were put on public display," and "investors understandably concluded that no bank's numbers were to be trusted."

**Why Wells specifically:** Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen, whom he compares to Tom Murphy and Dan Burke. Four shared traits:
1. Each pair is stronger than the sum of its parts because each partner understands, trusts and admires the other
2. Both pay able people well but abhor excess headcount
3. Both attack costs as vigorously when profits are at records as when under pressure
4. Both stick with what they understand

> "Thomas J. Watson Sr. of IBM followed the same rule: 'I'm no genius,' he said. 'I'm smart in spots — but I stay around those spots.'"

**The banking paradox resolved:** He opens by saying "The banking business is no favorite of ours" because 20:1 leverage means small asset mistakes destroy large equity. Then:

> "Because leverage of 20:1 magnifies the effects of managerial strengths and weaknesses, we have no interest in purchasing shares of a poorly-managed bank at a 'cheap' price. Instead, our only interest is in buying into well-managed banks at fair prices."

**The downside math:** He runs the stress test explicitly. Wells earns over $1 billion pre-tax after expensing $300M+ in loan losses. If **10% of all $48 billion in loans** hit problems and losses averaged **30% of principal** (including foregone interest), the company would roughly break even.

> "A year like that — which we consider only a low-level possibility, not a likelihood — would not distress us. In fact, at Berkshire we would love to acquire businesses or invest in capital projects that produced no return for a year, but that could then be expected to earn 20% on growing equity."

## The Falling-Price Philosophy

The philosophical centerpiece, triggered by the Wells Fargo decline:

> "Investors who expect to be ongoing buyers of investments throughout their lifetimes should adopt a similar attitude toward market fluctuations; instead many illogically become euphoric when stock prices rise and unhappy when they fall. They show no such confusion in their reaction to food prices: Knowing they are forever going to be buying food, they welcome falling prices and deplore price increases."

> "The most common cause of low prices is pessimism — sometimes pervasive, sometimes specific to a company or industry. We want to do business in such an environment, not because we like pessimism but because we like the prices it produces. It's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer."

**And the necessary guardrail against mindless contrarianism:**

> "None of this means, however, that a business or stock is an intelligent purchase simply because it is unpopular; a contrarian approach is just as foolish as a follow-the-crowd strategy. What's required is thinking rather than polling. Unfortunately, Bertrand Russell's observation about life in general applies with unusual force in the financial world: 'Most men would rather die than think. Many do.'"

## Junk Bonds: The Dagger on the Steering Wheel

Buffett's most sustained polemic since the 1989 zero-coupon essay.

**The dagger thesis and its refutation:**

> "The disciples of debt assured us that this collapse wouldn't happen: Huge debt, we were told, would cause operating managers to focus their efforts as never before, much as a dagger mounted on the steering wheel of a car could be expected to make its driver proceed with intensified care. We'll acknowledge that such an attention-getter would produce a very alert driver. But another certain consequence would be a deadly — and unnecessary — accident if the car hit even the tiniest pothole or sliver of ice. The roads of business are riddled with potholes; a plan that requires dodging them all is a plan for disaster."

**Margin of safety:** Graham's three words, "forty-two years after reading that, I still think those are the right three words."

**The statistical fallacy** — and the best line in the letter:

> "An assumption was being made that the universe of newly-minted junk bonds was identical to the universe of low-grade fallen angels... (That was an error similar to checking the historical death rate from Kool-Aid before drinking the version served at Jonestown.)"

**The two populations are different:**
- The fallen angel's manager "almost invariably yearned to regain investment-grade status and worked toward that goal"
- The junk-bond operator "devoted his energies not to finding a cure for his debt-ridden condition, but rather to finding another fix"
- And: "the fiduciary sensitivities of the executives managing the typical fallen angel were often... more finely developed than were those of the junk-bond-issuing **financiopath**"

**The Street's incentive:**

> "As usual, the Street's enthusiasm for an idea was proportional not to its merit, but rather to the revenue it would produce. Mountains of junk bonds were sold by those who didn't care to those who didn't think — and there was no shortage of either."

> "The only time to buy these is on a day with no 'y' in it."

But note the pragmatism: he *is* buying RJR Nabisco bonds — $440 million at yearend, since risen $150 million — because "the Company's credit is considerably better than was generally perceived" and the yield plus capital gain potential "more than compensates for the risk we incur (though that is far from nil)." He distinguishes old-fashioned fallen angels from manufactured junk, and he distinguishes RJR from the field.

## USAir: An Unforced Error

> "In making the USAir purchase, your Chairman displayed exquisite timing: I plunged into the business at almost the exact moment that it ran into severe problems. (No one pushed me; in tennis parlance, I committed an 'unforced error.')"

Two problems: post-merger integration of Piedmont (which he should have anticipated — "almost all airline mergers have been followed by operational turmoil") and industry economics.

> "The trouble this pricing has produced for all carriers illustrates an important truth: In a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor."

## The Convertible Preferred Valuation Correction

A useful public-service note: the press had calculated the preferreds as worth only their conversion value, implying the non-convertible preferred would be worth zero.

> "The point you should keep in mind is that most of the value of our convertible preferreds is derived from their fixed-income characteristics. That means the securities cannot be worth less than the value they would possess as non-convertible preferreds and may be worth more because of their conversion options."

## The Colman Mockler Tribute

> "No description better fitted Colman than 'gentleman' — a word signifying integrity, courage and modesty."

And the Forbes cover story connection: Gillette's success "has come not from marketing savvy... but has instead resulted from its devotion to quality. This mind-set has caused it to consistently focus its energies on coming up with something better, even though its existing products already ranked as the class of the field. In so depicting Gillette, Forbes in fact painted a portrait of Colman."

## Succession: Susan Buffett Nominated to the Board

Ken Chace steps down at 75 ("We have no mandatory retirement age for directors at Berkshire (and won't!)"). Susan Buffett nominated to succeed him. She is the second-largest shareholder and would inherit control.

> "Neither my estate plan nor that of my wife is designed to preserve the family fortune; instead, both are aimed at preserving the character of Berkshire and returning the fortune to society."

The three guarantees if he died tomorrow: no stock would have to be sold; a controlling shareholder and a like-minded manager would follow; and earnings would rise $1 million annually because Charlie would immediately sell The Indefensible.

## Appendix A: Graham's 1936 U.S. Steel Satire

This is a genuine find — written by Graham in 1936, given to Buffett in 1954, published here for the first time.

The premise: U.S. Steel announces a "sweeping modernization scheme." No changes to manufacturing or selling. Instead, the bookkeeping system is revamped:

1. **Write plant account down to minus $1 billion.** "As the plant wears out, the liability becomes correspondingly reduced. Hence, instead of the present depreciation charge of some $47,000,000 yearly there will be an annual appreciation credit of 5%, or $50,000,000. This will increase earnings by no less than $97,000,000 per annum."
2. **Reduce par value to 1¢; pay all wages in option warrants.** Payroll eliminated ($250M saved), employees enriched, and the company books a $49.99 gain per share subscribed — "in the interest of conservative accounting, however, this profit will not be included in the income account."
3. **Carry inventories at $1.** "The inventory on hand at the beginning of the year, valued at $1, will be sold during the year at an excellent profit."
4. **Replace preferred stock with non-interest-bearing bonds redeemable at 50% discount.** Turning dividend payments into "bond-retirement profit."
5. **Establish a $1 billion Contingency Reserve**, replenished from Capital Surplus generated by the warrant exercises.

**The pro forma result:** 1935 reported loss of **($2.76) per share** becomes **$49.80 per share**. The balance sheet total becomes **($774,999,999)** — negative.

And the closer:

> "It is the considered view of the Board of Directors that under the Modernization Scheme we shall be able to undersell all competitors to such a point that the anti-trust laws will constitute the only barrier to 100% domination of the industry."

**Why it's here:** Buffett's framing is that "funny business in accounting is not new" and that "excesses similar to those he then lampooned have many times since found their way into the financial statements of major American corporations and been duly certified by big-name auditors." The satire is from 1936; the 1980s junk-bond era is the same playbook with new props. Graham's joke is Buffett's indictment.

## Appendix B: "Some Thoughts on Selling Your Business"

This is the prototype of what later became a standalone document. Its structure:

**The asymmetry:** Owner-managers build a business over a lifetime of repetition and learning. They sell it once, "frequently in an emotionally-charged atmosphere," with brokers whose compensation is contingent on consummation. "Mistakes made in the once-in-a-lifetime sale of a business are not reversible."

**The two buyer types:**
1. **The strategic buyer** — "will usually have managers who feel they know how to run your business operations and, sooner or later, will want to apply some hands-on 'help.'" If larger, it has "squads of managers, recruited over the years in part by promises that they will get to run future acquisitions."
2. **The financial maneuverer** — "invariably operating with large amounts of borrowed money, who plans to resell either to the public or to another corporation as soon as the time is favorable. Frequently, this buyer's major contribution will be to change accounting methods so that earnings can be presented in the most favorable light just prior to his bailing out."

**Why Berkshire is different:**
- "We buy to keep, but we don't have, and don't expect to have, operating people in our parent organization."
- "In most cases, the managers of important businesses we have owned for many years have not been to Omaha or even met each other."
- "We have no one — family, recently recruited MBAs, etc. — to whom we have promised a chance to run businesses we have bought from owner-managers. And we won't have."
- "I'm enclosing a list of everyone from whom we have ever bought a business, and I invite you to check with them as to our performance versus our promises. You should be particularly interested in checking with the few whose businesses did not do well."
- **80/20 structure:** "We need 80% to consolidate earnings for tax purposes... It is equally important to us that the family members who run the business remain as owners. Very simply, we would not want to buy unless we felt key members of present management would stay on as our partners. Contracts cannot guarantee your continued interest; we would simply rely on your word."
- **What he actually does:** "capital allocation and selection and compensation of the top man. Other personnel decisions, operating strategies, etc. are his bailiwick."
- **Cash, no brokers, no re-trading:** "There would be no chance that a deal would be announced and that the buyer would then back off or start suggesting adjustments."
- **The honesty about wealth:** "It's only fair to tell you that you would be no richer after the sale than now. The ownership of your business already makes you wealthy and soundly invested. A sale would change the form of your wealth, but it wouldn't change its amount."

And the close:

> "I will not pester you; if you have any possible interest in selling, I would appreciate your call. I would be extraordinarily proud to have Berkshire, along with the key members of your family, own _______; I believe we would do very well financially; and I believe you would have just as much fun running the business over the next 20 years as you have had during the past 20."

This letter is the entire Berkshire acquisition pitch in one document. It's also a rare thing: a buyer telling a seller that the sale won't make him richer, that he should deal from strength because the business will be worth more later, and that he shouldn't be pressured.

## How Buffett's Mind Works (1990 Edition)

**1. He quantifies the intangible.** "Look-through earnings" is an abstract idea made concrete: $250M retained, less $30M hypothetical tax, plus $371M reported = $590M. He doesn't just assert that retained earnings matter; he computes them.

**2. He invents the metric that measures what matters.** The float cost table is genuinely original. Instead of quoting the combined ratio like everyone else, he asks: *what did this float cost us, and is that cheaper than government borrowing?* The answer — cheaper in 19 of 24 years — is the real case for Berkshire's insurance business.

**3. He admits a thesis was wrong.** The media section is a rare public retraction. He spent years calling newspapers economic marvels. Now he says the pricing power was structural, the structure changed, and intrinsic value is permanently lower. He doesn't blame the recession; he identifies the increase in advertising channels as the permanent cause.

**4. He runs the stress test out loud.** On Wells Fargo, he doesn't just say "it's cheap." He calculates what happens if 10% of all loans go bad at 30% severity — and says he'd welcome that year. That's what a margin of safety looks like in practice.

**5. He sees the incentive problem behind the sales pitch.** The junk-bond critique isn't about credit analysis; it's about *who benefits from the transaction.* "Mountains of junk bonds were sold by those who didn't care to those who didn't think." The dagger analogy and the Jonestown line both attack the same thing: a theory that justifies the seller's fee.

**6. He distinguishes between the product and the distribution.** On convertible preferreds, he corrects the press: the value is mostly fixed-income, not conversion. On media, the product is still excellent; the distribution channel's pricing power is what broke. He separates the durable from the perishable.

**7. He plans for his own absence — and jokes about it.** The succession section is serious: Susan on the board, no stock sales required, a like-minded manager to follow. Then: "Berkshire's earnings would increase by $1 million annually, since Charlie would immediately sell our corporate jet."

## The Key Quote

> "What's required is thinking rather than polling."

That's the whole letter in six words. The Wells Fargo purchase required thinking against a panicked market. The junk-bond avoidance required thinking against a euphoric one. The media reassessment required thinking against his own prior conviction. The look-through framework required thinking against GAAP. The float table required thinking against the industry's standard metric.

But the passage that best captures what this letter *is* — a teaching document disguised as an annual report — might be this one:

> "An investor cannot obtain superior profits from stocks by simply committing to a specific investment category or style. He can earn them only by carefully evaluating facts and continuously exercising discipline."

He said it in 1989 about arbitrage. He's demonstrating it in 1990 across every asset class he touches. And the two appendices — Graham's satire and the letter to a seller — are the curriculum: here is what accounting fraud looks like, and here is what an honest buyer looks like.
